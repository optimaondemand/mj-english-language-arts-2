Lesson 1.02

Building Suspense: What We Know vs. What We Fear

Interactive Lesson ~14 min
🎯 WHAT YOU WILL BE ABLE TO DO
  • 1
    Explain what pacing means and describe how a writer controls it.
  • 2
    Identify foreshadowing and explain what it adds to a story.
  • 3
    Describe how the gap between what the reader knows and what the character fears creates suspense.
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS

The middle of “The Most Dangerous Game” is a masterclass in pacing. Zaroff tells Rainsford exactly what will happen. The reader sees it coming. But the way Connell stretches, slows, and accelerates the hunt is what makes the story unforgettable. Today you analyze the tools he uses: pacing, foreshadowing, and the gap between what the reader knows and what the character fears.

Materials: The Most Dangerous Game PDF (linked in Lesson 1.01) · Short Story Reading Journal
⚡ THREE TOOLS OF SUSPENSE
Pacing
How fast or slow a story moves. Short sentences create speed and urgency. Long, winding sentences slow the reader down. Gaps and silences stretch time until it becomes unbearable.
“He ran. He stumbled. He ran again.” Three beats. Three pauses. The reader feels each one.
Foreshadowing
Clues planted early that hint at what comes later. The reader feels something coming. The character may not notice at all. The gap between what the reader senses and what the character misses is where dread lives.
A detail that seems small in chapter one becomes enormous in chapter three. The writer put it there on purpose.
The Knowledge Gap
When the reader knows (or suspects) something the character does not. That gap creates a particular kind of dread: the reader watches the character move toward something terrible and cannot warn them.
You have figured out the danger. The character has not. Every step they take feels worse than the last.

📖 READ & NOTICE: THREE MOMENTS FROM THE MIDDLE

Here are three moments from the hunt section of “The Most Dangerous Game.” Read each one. Notice what Connell is doing. Add your answers to the Week 1 section of your Reading Journal.

Moment A: Zaroff’s Dinner Conversation

Zaroff describes hunting with terrifying calm before Rainsford fully understands what is being said.

“I have invented a new sensation. I have invented a new animal to hunt. May I show you my new collection of heads? Will you have another glass of Chablis?”

What does Zaroff know that he is not quite saying yet? What is Rainsford still missing? Write a sentence or two in your Reading Journal about what is happening in this moment.

✅ Add to Reading Journal: Week 1, Moment A.
Moment B: Rainsford in the Jungle, Night 1 (The Malay Man-Catcher)

After Rainsford sets his trap and waits, Connell writes:

“Rainsford did not want to believe what his reason told him was true, but the truth was as evident as the sun that had by now pushed through the morning mists.”

What does Connell slow down in this sentence, and why? Notice how the long, heavy sentence moves compared to an action sentence. What effect does that pace have on the reader?

✅ Add to Reading Journal: Week 1, Moment B.
Moment C: The Third Night

Zaroff ends the hunt early and walks back to his chateau. Rainsford follows. A trap springs. Ivan is killed. Connell writes:

“He heard it as he heard the sound of the sea. Then he did not hear it anymore.”

How does Connell use short sentences here to control the reader’s experience? What does the shortness of those sentences do to time? Write a sentence about what those two lines accomplish.

✅ Add to Reading Journal: Week 1, Moment C.
