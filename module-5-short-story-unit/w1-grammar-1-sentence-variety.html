Sentence Variety: Short and Long Sentences

~12 min
🎯 TODAY’S FOCUS
Short sentences hit hard. Long sentences build and layer. A writer who knows how to mix them can speed your heart up or slow it down. Today you will see how sentence length controls tension in a suspense story, and you will practice mixing sentence lengths in your own writing.
💡 TWO LENGTHS, TWO EFFECTS
Short
“He lived.” Richard Connell, “The Most Dangerous Game” (1924, public domain) Effect: The reader stops. The world stops. Then everything continues. Two words carry the full weight of everything that came before them. Short sentences create urgency, surprise, and finality.
Long + Layered
“Even so zealous a hunter as General Zaroff could not trace him there, he told himself; his training had taught him that a still hunter could outwait a moving one.” Richard Connell, “The Most Dangerous Game” (1924, public domain) Effect: We feel the tangle of the moment: a mind working fast while the body struggles. Long sentences layer detail and stretch out time, letting dread build while we wait to find out what happens.

Both are from the same story. Both are doing work. The choice of length is a craft decision.

⚡ HOW IT WORKS
  • 1 A very short sentence placed after a long one makes the reader feel a jolt. It is the sentence equivalent of a jump cut in film. The contrast is what creates the effect.
  • 2 A long sentence can hold back information, giving us detail while we wait to find out what happens. The reader’s mind fills in the tension during all that delay.
  • 3 In suspense writing, sentence length is part of the pacing. To understand any sentence you work with, ask: “What is being said about [the subject]?” to find the verb. Then ask “Who or what [verb]?” to find the subject. Once you know the core of the sentence, you can see clearly how much the writer has added or stripped away.

Worked example: a short sentence

Classified sentence
He subject pronoun
lived action verb
.

Two parts. Nothing extra. The subject pronoun is He (ask: “Who lived?”). The action verb is lived (ask: “What is being said about He?”). Connell stripped everything away to create maximum impact.

Worked example: finding the core of a long sentence

Sentence: “Rainsford had not entirely forgotten the trap he had made.” (core only)
Rainsford subject noun
had helping verb
forgotten main verb
trap object noun
...

The subject noun is Rainsford (ask: “Who had forgotten?”). The helping verb is had, the main verb is forgotten (ask: “What is being said about Rainsford?”). Everything else in the full sentence is layered on top of this core.

☐ CHECK-IN
Think about a scary or tense scene you have read or watched. Did the story slow down or speed up right at the scariest moment?
☐ PRACTICE: SHORT OR LONG?
Which sentence type would work better in each situation? Pick Short or Long, then read the feedback.
A hunter hears a twig snap behind him.
The writer wants to describe the jungle in detail while the hunter slowly realizes he is being watched.
The hunter finally escapes after a desperate chase. The writer wants to mark that moment with maximum weight.

  • One LONG sentence describing what he sees and feels as he swims toward it.
