Punctuation for Pacing: Commas, Dashes, and Ellipses

Grammar Lesson ~12 min
💡 TODAY’S FOCUS

Punctuation is not just about following rules. In suspense writing, it controls how fast the reader moves. A comma forces a pause. A dash — like this one — interrupts. An ellipsis trails off into silence... Richard Connell uses each one to speed up, slow down, or hold the reader in place at exactly the right moment.

⚡ THREE MARKS, THREE EFFECTS
,   COMMA
Slows the reader
Creates micro-pauses inside a sentence. The reader stops, holds, moves forward. Multiple commas in a row slow the sentence down to almost nothing.
“He waited, listening, hardly breathing.”
The sentence imitates the stillness of waiting. Each comma is a held breath.
—   DASH
Interrupts or inserts
The em dash creates a sharp break in the sentence. It can interrupt a thought, insert a sudden detail, or snap the reader’s attention sideways, the way a noise interrupts thinking.
“He had not entirely forgotten the trap — the Malay man-catcher — when he lurched savagely downward.”
The dashes interrupt the sentence with extra detail. The syntax mirrors what happens in a crisis: one thought cuts in on another.
...   ELLIPSIS
Trails off
An ellipsis suggests something unfinished, unsaid, or uncertain. The sentence does not end: it fades. The reader is left in the gap, which is exactly where a suspense writer wants them.
“He heard something... and then nothing.”
The gap after the ellipsis is where imagination takes over. The writer stops. The reader keeps going.
📖 HOW IT WORKS
The Comma for Pacing

Read this sentence aloud: “He waited, listening, hardly breathing.”

The commas break the sentence into three separate beats. Each one is a pause, a moment of stillness. Read it again and notice the three stops, just as a person holding their breath stops. Each comma extends the silence. Connell is not just describing waiting. He is making the reader wait.

📋 SENTENCE: “He waited, listening, hardly breathing.”
He Subject pronoun
waited Verb (action)
, Pause
listening Adverb (How?)
, Pause
hardly Adverb (To what extent?)
breathing Verb (action)
Three beats. Three pauses. The sentence imitates the act of holding your breath.
The Dash for Interruption

The em dash (—) can do two things: insert a sudden thought into the middle of a sentence, or create a sharp break that stops the reader cold.

In Connell’s sentence, the dashes work like a memory snapping forward:

“He had not entirely forgotten the trap — the Malay man-catcher — when he lurched savagely downward.”

The sentence is moving forward, then the dashes cut in with the name of the trap. That interruption mirrors what happens in Rainsford’s mind: the crisis is happening, and the name of the thing surfaces in the middle of it. The syntax and the experience match each other.

The Ellipsis for Trailing Off

An ellipsis (...) tells the reader: the sentence could have continued, but it did not. Something was left out. Something is unresolved.

In a suspense story, the ellipsis creates a gap. The writer stops the sentence. The reader’s imagination fills in what comes next. Consider:

“He heard something... and then nothing.”

The ellipsis stretches the silence. The reader hangs in that pause, wondering what was heard and why it stopped. That is exactly where a suspense writer wants the reader: in the gap between knowing and not knowing.
