Pronoun-Antecedent Agreement: Matching Pronouns to the Nouns They Replace
Lesson ≈12 min
📝 TODAY'S FOCUS
A pronoun stands in for a noun. Today you will learn the rule that makes pronouns work: a pronoun must match the noun it replaces in number and gender. You will be able to spot agreement errors and fix them.
📚 SENTENCE FROM THE BOOK (Chapter 17)
“She looked up and saw her.”
⚡ WARM-UP
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before this lesson: when you read “She looked up and saw her,” do you automatically know who each pronoun refers to? (No wrong answer.)
💡 THE ONE RULE TO REMEMBER
ANTECEDENT
The noun the pronoun replaces.
Example: Marguerite
→
PRONOUN
The word that stands in for the noun.
Examples: she, her
Agreement Rule: A pronoun must agree with its antecedent in number (one person or thing: she/he/it; more than one: they/them) and in gender (she for female; he for male; it for a thing or animal).
📚 HOW TO FIND THE ANTECEDENT

In the sentence “She looked up and saw her,” there are two pronouns: she and her. To use pronouns correctly, you need to identify what noun each one stands in for. That noun is the antecedent.

  1. Find the verb, then ask who did it.  “What is being said about she?” She looked. “Who or what looked?” She. The subject pronoun is she. Look at the surrounding lines in Ch. 17: Marguerite went forward a few steps. So she refers to Marguerite. Marguerite is the antecedent.
  2. Find the second pronoun.  “She tore open the envelope and looked at her”: actually, in our sentence: “She looked up and saw her.” Who did she see? The context tells us she saw another woman (Lady Blakeney saw the other figure). Both she and her are singular and feminine, matching the antecedent Marguerite and the person observed.
  3. Check agreement.  Marguerite is one person and female. She = singular feminine. Her = singular feminine. The pronouns agree with their antecedents. No error.
