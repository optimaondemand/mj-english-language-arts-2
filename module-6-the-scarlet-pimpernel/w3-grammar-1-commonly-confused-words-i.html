Commonly Confused Words I: their / there / they're and your / you're
Lesson ≈12 min
📝 TODAY'S FOCUS
Six words, two sets, one principle: a possessive pronoun never gets an apostrophe, and a contraction always does. By the end of this lesson you will be able to use their, there, they're, your, and you're correctly every time you write.
📚 THIS WEEK'S SENTENCE (from Chapter 7)
"She tried to pierce the distance far away, beyond which lay the shores of France: that relentless and stern France which was exacting her pound of flesh, the blood-tax from the noblest of her sons."

💡 THE ONE RULE TO REMEMBER
Possessive pronouns never have apostrophes.
Contractions always do.
their / there / they're
their = belongs to them (possessive pronoun)
Armand placed his hand over their linked arms.

there = a place, or used to introduce a sentence
She stood there on the cliff for an hour.

they're = they are (contraction)
They're sailing before the wind toward France.
your / you're
your = belongs to you (possessive pronoun)
"Remember, dear, I have only you to care for me." Your life is precious to me.

you're = you are (contraction)
"You're my own brave sister," he said gently.
The quick test: replace the word with the full two-word phrase. If it still makes sense, use the apostrophe version. If not, use the plain possessive. Try it: "Check you're/your notes." Replace: "Check you are notes." Does that make sense? No. So the correct word is your.
📚 THE SENTENCE FROM THE BOOK, CLASSIFIED

Look at an original example sentence using this week's words, modeled on the kinds of sentences Orczy writes. Watch how the classification works.

"Their ship was gone, and they're sailing toward danger now; there is nothing she can do."
Classified sentence:
Their
possessive pronoun ship
subject noun was
verb gone,
  and
conjunction they're
contraction = they are sailing
main verb toward
preposition danger
noun (obj. of prep.) now;
adverb there
place / intro word is
verb nothing
noun she
subject pronoun can do.
verb phrase

Notice: Their is a possessive pronoun (no apostrophe). They're has an apostrophe because it stands for "they are." There introduces the second clause: it is neither a possessive nor a contraction.
