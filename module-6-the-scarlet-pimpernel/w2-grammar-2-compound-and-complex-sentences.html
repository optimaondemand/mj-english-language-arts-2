Compound and Complex Sentences

📝 TODAY'S FOCUS
A complete sentence has a subject and a verb. When two complete sentences are joined with a conjunction like and, but, or so, the result is a compound sentence. When one complete sentence is attached to a dependent clause (a group of words with a subject and verb that cannot stand alone), the result is a complex sentence. Today you will tell them apart, using sentences straight from Chapters 3 through 6.

💡 THE TWO STRUCTURES TO KNOW
Compound sentence
Two independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (and, but, or, so, for, yet, nor) or a semicolon. Each half can stand alone.
Everyone heard it, [independent] and everyone paused. [independent]
Complex sentence
One independent clause plus one or more dependent clauses, joined by a subordinating conjunction (because, although, when, if, since, while, after, before, unless). The dependent clause cannot stand alone.
Although she was insulted, [dependent] Marguerite kept her composure. [independent]
📚 CLASSIFYING THIS WEEK'S SENTENCE

“Everyone in the coffee-room heard it and paused instinctively, listening to it for a moment.”

  1. What is being said about everyone?  Everyone heard and paused. Two verbs, both in the predicate.
  2. Who or what heard and paused?  Everyone. Subject noun.
  3. Can the first half stand alone?  Yes: “Everyone in the coffee-room heard it.” That is an independent clause.
  4. Can the second half stand alone?  Yes: “Everyone paused instinctively.” That is also an independent clause.
  5. How are they joined?  The coordinating conjunction and. That makes this a compound sentence.
