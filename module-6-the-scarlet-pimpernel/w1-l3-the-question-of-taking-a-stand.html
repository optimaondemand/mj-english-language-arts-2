The Question of Taking a Stand: What Chapters 1 and 2 Are Really Asking
Lesson ≈12 min
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
By the end of Chapter 1, a mysterious Englishman has risked everything for a countess and her children. Nobody asked him to. Nobody could make him stop. This lesson asks: what does the novel seem to believe about that kind of courage?
Today you pull back from the plot and look at what Chapters 1 and 2 set up thematically. Then you prepare your Quick Write: your first attempt at answering the module question in your own words.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what theme means and identify the theme Orczy is building in Chapters 1 and 2.
2
I will be able to begin forming my own position on the module question, supported by evidence from the first two chapters.
💡 THEME VS. PLOT
Plot
What happens. In Ch. 1: Bibot guards the barricade; he tells the story of Grospierre; a cart gets through and the Comtesse de Tourney escapes.
Theme
The idea the story is exploring. In Chs. 1 and 2: what does it mean to take a stand for others at personal risk? When is that courage, and when is it something else?
A theme is a question the novel keeps returning to, not a one-sentence moral. Orczy does not tell you what to think. She shows you what is at stake and lets the question build.
📚 PART 1 · READ & NOTICE: WHAT ORCZY SHOWS US
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
After Chapters 1 and 2, where do you stand on the module question: when is it right to risk everything for strangers?

Now look at what the two chapters actually give you as evidence. Both sides of the argument are already there in the text.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH SIDE DOES EACH DETAIL SUPPORT?
For each detail from Chapters 1 and 2, decide whether it supports the idea that risking for strangers is justified, or the idea that it is not justified (or extremely dangerous). Think about it before you choose.
1. The Comtesse de Tourney and her children escape through the West Gate disguised in the old hag's cart (Ch. 1).
2. Sergeant Grospierre was sent to the guillotine for allowing aristos to escape (Ch. 1).
3. At the Fisherman's Rest, some English people think France's crisis is none of their business, while Lord Tony and Sir Andrew are already helping (Ch. 2).
