Sentence Variety: Simple, Compound, and Complex Sentences

📝 TODAY'S FOCUS
A paragraph made up of sentences that are all the same length and structure sounds flat. Orczy mixes short, punchy sentences with long, rolling ones. That mixture is what gives her prose energy. Today you will name the three main sentence types and see how they do different jobs.

💡 THE THREE SENTENCE TYPES
Simple sentence
One independent clause: one subject noun or subject pronoun + one predicate (verb). It stands on its own.
From Ch. 2: “Sally looked cross for a moment.”
Subject noun: Sally. Verb: looked. One complete thought.
Compound sentence
Two or more independent clauses joined by a conjunction (and, but, or, so, yet) or a semicolon. Each part could stand alone.
From Ch. 2: “Bibot laughed so much at his own tale, and he could not continue for some time.”
Two independent clauses joined by and.
Complex sentence
One independent clause plus one or more dependent clauses (beginning with words like because, although, when, if, since, while, that). The dependent clause cannot stand alone.
From Ch. 2: “Portly in build, jovial in countenance, Mr. Jellyband was a typical John Bull of those days.”
The opening phrase modifies the subject noun and depends on the main clause to complete it.
📚 HOW TO CLASSIFY: ASK ABOUT THE CLAUSE

A clause has its own subject and verb. An independent clause is a complete thought that stands alone. A dependent clause has a subject and verb but is not a complete thought by itself (it usually starts with a conjunction like because, when, although, or that). Watch how it works on this week’s sentences.

  1. Who or what is the sentence about?  Sally. She is the subject noun.
  2. What is being said about Sally?  looked. The verb is looked. One independent clause. This is a simple sentence.
  3. Now the second sentence: The opening phrase (“Portly in build, jovial in countenance...”) describes the subject noun Mr. Jellyband but cannot stand alone as a sentence. The main clause is “Mr. Jellyband was...a typical rural John Bull.” The descriptive opening depends on that main clause. This is a complex sentence.
Label the first sentence:
Sally
subject noun looked
verb (action verb) cross for a moment. simple sentence

Tip for your writing: one short simple sentence after a long complex one creates a punch. Orczy uses this throughout Chapter 2. Notice it when you reread.
