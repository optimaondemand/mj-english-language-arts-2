📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Chapters 3 and 4 introduce the man everyone thinks Sir Percy Blakeney is. By the end of Chapter 6 you will suspect he is something else entirely. Today you will study how Orczy builds that gap.
You will read Chapters 3 and 4 and look at how the author uses physical description, speech, and other characters' reactions to build a portrait of Percy as an empty-headed socialite. You will also start asking: what is this portrait hiding?

💡 THE KEY IDEA
Characterization is the way a writer builds a person on the page. There are five main methods. See how many Orczy uses on Percy in just two chapters.
Appearance
What the character looks like: “femininely white” hands, immaculate coat
Speech
What the character says: “Demmed climate this” · “Lud!”
Actions
What the character does: yawns, uses an eyeglass, refuses a duel
Others' reactions
What other characters think or say about them
Narrator's comment
What the narrator tells us directly about the person
📚 TWO WORDS FOR YOUR TOOLKIT
Those five methods split into two kinds, and the difference is who does the work: the narrator, or you.
Direct characterization
The narrator simply tells you. Chapter 6 states that society considers Percy wealthy, fashionable and hopelessly stupid. You are not working it out; you are being told.
Indirect characterization
You infer it yourself from what a character looks like, says, does, thinks, or from how other people react. Four of the five methods above work this way.
Both are at work on Percy at once, and they disagree with each other. That disagreement is the whole point of the disguise, and it is what you will track this week.

📚 PART 1 · READ & NOTICE: READ CHAPTERS 3 AND 4

Read Chapters 3 and 4 now. In Chapter 3, French refugees arrive at the Fisherman's Rest, escorted by Lord Antony Dewhurst and Sir Andrew Ffoulkes. The League of the Scarlet Pimpernel is named for the first time. The Comtesse reveals that Marguerite, now Lady Blakeney, once denounced the Marquis de St. Cyr to the revolutionary tribunal. In Chapter 4, the chaotic arrival of Lord Antony’s guests creates an awkward collision between the Comtesse and Lady Blakeney. The Comtesse’s cold rebuff and Marguerite’s sharp composure are both on full display.

As you read, watch for how Orczy describes the French refugees and how she describes the English characters who help them. Notice the contrast between the gravity of what the League does and the lightness with which Lord Antony describes it as “sport.”

💡 KEEP THIS IN MIND
In Chapter 4, Lord Antony says the League rescues people purely for “sport.” The Comtesse cannot believe it. As you read her reaction, ask: what does the gap between his words and her disbelief suggest about the real motive?
⚡ TRY IT · WHICH METHOD IS ORCZY USING?

Each passage below is from Chapters 3 or 4. Decide which characterization method Orczy is using. Think it over before you click.

1. “Lord Antony Dewhurst...was in those days a very perfect type of a young English gentleman, tall, well-setup, broad of shoulders and merry of face.” (Ch. 3)
2. “Suzanne, I forbid you to speak to that woman,” said the Comtesse. (Ch. 4)
3. “Sport, Madame la Comtesse, sport...we are a nation of sportsmen, and just now it is the fashion to pull the hare from between the teeth of the hound.” (Ch. 4)
📚 PART 3 · READ & NOTICE: A DETAIL THAT DOESN'T FIT

Near the end of Chapter 4, as the Blakeney coach arrives and Lord Antony scrambles to manage the awkward collision of guests, Sir Andrew Ffoulkes notices something. He watches Percy open the door for Marguerite, and then:

“Only Sir Andrew Ffoulkes...noted the curious look of intense longing, of deep and hopeless passion, with which the inane and flippant Sir Percy followed the retreating figure of his brilliant wife.” (Ch. 6)

An inane, flippant man does not look at anyone with “intense longing” and “deep passion.” Orczy plants this one moment where the mask slips, and only one character catches it.

✏️ READING JOURNAL · ADD THIS TO THE WEEK 2 SECTION

Answer each question in a few sentences. Point to the text for each answer.

  1. In Chapters 3 and 4, name two things Orczy does to make the reader think Percy is a lightweight. Describe the detail, and say which characterization method it is.
  2. Why does only Sir Andrew notice the look on Percy’s face at the end? What does it mean that the narrator gives this observation to Sir Andrew rather than stating it directly?
  3. The Comtesse calls what Marguerite did “wicked.” Lord Antony and Sir Andrew are uncertain. Whose account should we trust? Explain your thinking.

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Orczy wants you to laugh at Percy Blakeney and to feel certain he is an idiot. Hold that certainty loosely. The novel is about a man who has made a career of being underestimated.
