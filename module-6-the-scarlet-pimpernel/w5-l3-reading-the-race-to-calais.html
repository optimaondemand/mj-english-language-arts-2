Lesson 5.03
Reading the Race to Calais: How Orczy Builds Suspense
≈12 min
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In Ch. 21, Marguerite races to Dover to cross to France. The storm stops her for an entire night. Why would a novelist slow down the action at the moment readers are most eager to move forward?
Today you look at how Orczy uses delay and setting details to build suspense. You will be able to explain one technique Orczy uses to keep readers anxious.

💡 THE ONE IDEA TO HOLD ONTO
Orczy withholds the crossing for an entire chapter. That is not an accident: delay is how a novelist makes readers feel urgency.
1
Marguerite arrives at Fisherman's Rest: midnight
The coast is closed. A storm is raging.
2
Sir Andrew arrives
They wait. Both sides wait.
3
Morning: storm has abated
Another delay. The tide is out.
4
Midday: Chauvelin possibly already ahead
Every hour means danger.
5
5 p.m.: they sail
Fourteen hours lost.

📚 PART 2 · READ & NOTICE: THE DELAY

In Ch. 21, Marguerite does something unusually brave: she sits in the Fisherman's Rest at midnight, alone, listening to the storm, and waits. The grandfather clock ticks. The breakers crash. Orczy lingers on all of this. The chapter is called ‘Suspense.’ It works because Orczy gives us Marguerite sitting still while everything outside rages. That stillness is not weakness. It is Orczy making us feel the weight of every lost hour.

In Ch. 22, when Marguerite finally lands in Calais and walks through the muddy streets to the Chat Gris, Orczy makes a point of describing the tavern in detail: squalid, broken, badly lit. Then she gives us the one piece of hope. Percy ordered supper and will return. That is hope. But the chapter ends there, on that small fact, with everything still uncertain. That is how suspense works: give the reader just enough hope to keep them afraid.
