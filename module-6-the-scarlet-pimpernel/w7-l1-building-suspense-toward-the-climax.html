📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Three groups of people are all moving through the same dark, coastal night toward the same hidden hut. None of them can fully see the others. How does Orczy use that situation to make you afraid for everyone at once?
Today you will read Chapters 27 and 28, the long tense chase across the French coast, and learn how Orczy builds suspense in the climax by controlling what each character knows and does not know.

💡 THE ONE IDEA TO HOLD ONTO: HOW SUSPENSE WORKS
🔑
Information Gap
The reader knows something a character does not, or a character knows something another one does not.
+
⌛
Ticking Clock
Something bad is approaching. The characters are moving toward it without being able to stop.
+
🌛
Darkness and Silence
Sensory details (dark roads, faint sounds, hidden figures) keep the reader uncertain about what is coming.
When an author knows more than the characters know, and gives the reader that extra knowledge, the fear of what the character cannot see is what produces suspense.

📚 PART 2 · READ & NOTICE: WHAT ORCZY IS DOING

The chase through the dark

In Chapters 27 and 28, three groups move toward the same small hut on the French coast: Chauvelin and his soldiers closing in from behind, Marguerite following on foot through the darkness, and the Scarlet Pimpernel somewhere ahead, unseen. Orczy creates suspense by keeping each of these groups partially blind to the others. Chauvelin does not know Marguerite is behind him. Marguerite does not know Percy is disguised as the Jewish cart driver, Benjamin Rosenbaum, sitting right in front of her. The reader knows more than any one character. That gap between what the reader knows and what each character can see is dramatic irony, and it is Orczy’s main tool in these chapters. She reinforces it with sensory details of darkness and silence: the rumble of cart wheels ahead, the sound of soldiers’ boots approaching from behind, the distant murmur of the sea, and the moonlight that appears and disappears like a threat. Every small sound Marguerite hears becomes a clue that tightens the net around Percy, who is, in fact, the very driver carrying Chauvelin straight to his own hiding place.

💡 WORTH NOTING
Chapter 27’s title is "On the Track." Chapter 28’s is "The Père Blanchard’s Hut." Both titles are Chauvelin’s perspective. Orczy chose titles that make us feel Chauvelin gaining ground. That choice is itself a craft decision about suspense.
📚 PART 3 · READ & NOTICE: NOW READ CHAPTERS 27–28

Read Chapters 27 and 28 now. In Chapter 27, Marguerite leaves the Chat Gris on foot and follows Chauvelin’s cart in the dark. She overhears the soldier’s report: Armand and the other fugitives are waiting at a hut halfway down the cliffs near Miquelon. Chauvelin gathers his soldiers and moves toward the hut. In Chapter 28, Marguerite follows the soldiers along the footpath, loses her shoes, and finally sees the Daydream on the moonlit sea. She descends the cliffs and tries to reach the hut to warn Percy. Chauvelin catches her and gags her, threatening to shoot Armand if she makes a sound.
