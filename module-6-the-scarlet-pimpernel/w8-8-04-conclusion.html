📖 WHAT THIS LESSON IS FOR
The conclusion does not repeat the thesis. It lands it.
A conclusion that only restates what you already said wastes the reader's time. A conclusion that lands the argument pushes the reader to see something they did not see before. Today you write the conclusion point for your outline: two or three sentences that restate your argument in fresh words and say why it matters beyond the novel.

💡 WHAT A CONCLUSION DOES AND DOES NOT DO
Here is a clear comparison. A strong conclusion does the right-hand column, not the left.
A WEAK CONCLUSION... A STRONG CONCLUSION...
Copies the thesis sentence word for word Restates the position in different words, showing growth from the argument
Lists the evidence again Says what the evidence adds up to: what it reveals about the novel or about courage in general
Stops at the plot level ("so the Pimpernel saved the aristocrats") Steps back and says what the novel is asking readers to think about courage, heroism, or disguise beyond just this story
Ends with a vague summary like "In conclusion, I have shown that..." Ends with a sentence that feels like an arrival: the reader understands something they did not fully see at the start
📚 READ AND NOTICE: A WORKED EXAMPLE

Here is a conclusion point written for the same essay as the earlier examples. Notice that it does not repeat the thesis, it does not list evidence again, and it says what the argument means beyond the plot. This is the model for the moves, not an answer to copy.

RESTATE IN FRESH WORDS The Scarlet Pimpernel does not celebrate the easiest kind of courage: the kind that earns applause. It celebrates the kind that earns contempt.
WHAT IT ADDS UP TO Everything the Pimpernel sacrifices, the admiration of his wife, his reputation as a serious man, the chance to be recognized, is given up precisely because the mission only works if no one suspects him.
WHY IT MATTERS BEYOND THE STORY Orczy asks whether heroism that is invisible is still heroism. The answer the novel gives, quietly, across every disguise Percy wears, is yes: sometimes courage means letting the world think you are a fool.

The last sentence feels like an arrival. It says something true about the novel that the reader might carry beyond the page. Your conclusion does not need to be this long. It needs to reach that quality: a sentence that lands, not just wraps up.

⚡ CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

For each sentence, decide: does it land the argument (strong), or does it only repeat or list (weak)?

1. “In conclusion, I have shown that the Pimpernel’s disguise is heroic because it requires moral courage and the willingness to sacrifice reputation.”
2. “Orczy suggests that courage is not always recognized as courage at the time: the bravest act in the novel goes unnoticed by almost everyone who witnesses it.”
3. “To summarize, Percy risked his marriage, used a disguise to fool Chauvelin, and showed that the League’s secrecy was necessary.”
4. “The novel leaves readers with a question worth keeping: what do we owe to people who protect us at a cost they cannot explain and we will never know?”
🧠 NOW ADD YOUR CONCLUSION TO THE OUTLINE

Open your Quick Write 7 and your outline document. Below your counterargument section, add a conclusion point. Write two or three sentences that restate your position in fresh words, say what the argument adds up to, and leave the reader with something to carry. Use the table and the worked example as your guide.

When you finish, read the whole outline from top to bottom. Thesis, evidence points, counterargument, conclusion. Does each part serve the argument? Does the conclusion land it?
