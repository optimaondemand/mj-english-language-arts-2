📖 WHAT THIS LESSON IS FOR
A strong argument does not ignore the other side. It faces it and answers it.
This lesson names the strongest objection to the thesis, states it fairly, and writes a response that shows why the argument still holds. A counterargument is not a flaw in an essay. It is a sign of careful reading. The lesson ends with this section added to the outline document.

💡 THE THREE MOVES
A counterargument section in the outline needs three moves in order.
MOVE 1 · STATE THE OTHER SIDE FAIRLY
Name the strongest objection to the thesis. If the thesis argues the disguise is heroic, state the strongest reason someone might think it is deceptive or wrong. If it argues the disguise is deceptive, state the strongest reason someone might think it is heroic. Use language like: "One could argue that..." or "A reader might point out that..." Do not set up a weak version of the other side just to knock it down easily.
MOVE 2 · GRANT WHAT IS TRUE
Acknowledge what is right about the objection. Language like: "This is a fair point" or "It is true that..." Readers trust an arguer more when they see that the arguer knows the other side has some merit. Skipping this move makes a response look as if the objection was not really understood.
MOVE 3 · EXPLAIN WHY THE ARGUMENT STILL HOLDS
Use language like: "However..." or "Even so..." Then explain why the objection does not overturn the thesis. The other side is not completely wrong; the argument is still the better reading of what Orczy shows.
📚 READ AND NOTICE: A WORKED EXAMPLE

Here is a complete counterargument section written for an essay arguing the disguise is heroic. Notice all three moves and how they connect. This is a model for the structure, not an answer to copy.

MOVE 1: OTHER SIDE One could argue that the Pimpernel's disguise is deceptive rather than heroic, because it requires lying not only to his enemies but also to Marguerite, the person he claims to love most.
MOVE 2: GRANT This is a fair point. The novel does show that Marguerite suffers genuine hurt from Percy's apparent coldness, and that pain is real, not a minor cost.
MOVE 3: RESPONSE However, Orczy makes clear that Percy keeps the secret specifically to protect Marguerite from Chauvelin's threats, not out of disrespect or indifference. The deception is not the point of the disguise; it is the price of it. The novel asks readers to see that some forms of love require a sacrifice the other person cannot even know about, and that sacrifice is what makes the disguise heroic rather than merely dishonest.

Notice that Move 3 does not just repeat the thesis. It answers the specific objection and explains why the evidence still supports the original argument. A response should do the same.

⚡ CHECK UNDERSTANDING

For each sentence, identify which of the three counterargument moves it is performing.

1. “However, Orczy consistently frames the League’s secrecy as the condition of its effectiveness: exposure means deaths, not just arrests.”
2. “A reader might argue that the Pimpernel’s secrecy is a form of arrogance: he decides alone that Marguerite cannot be trusted with the truth.”
3. “It is true that Percy’s silence causes Marguerite real suffering, and the novel does not minimize that cost.”
🔍 CHECK THE REASONING BEFORE WRITING
Module 2 covered three ways an argument can go wrong: attacking the person instead of the argument, offering a false either/or when more real options exist, and making a hasty generalization from just one example. Those same problems can creep into a counterargument section. Check the Move 1 objection: is it the strongest real version of the opposing view, or has it quietly turned into a weak, easy-to-beat version (a straw man) or an attack on a person instead of an idea? Fix Move 1 before writing Moves 2 and 3 if it needs to be fairer.
🧠 ADD THE COUNTERARGUMENT TO THE OUTLINE

Open Quick Write 5 and the outline document. Below the evidence points, add a counterargument section with all three moves. Label each move in the outline so each is easy to find when the essay is written next week.

Pick the strongest objection, not the easiest one. If the response can handle the strongest version, the argument is solid.
