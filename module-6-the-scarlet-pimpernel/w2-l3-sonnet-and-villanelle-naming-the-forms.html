Sonnet and Villanelle: Naming the Forms

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In Chapters 3 and 4, Orczy builds Sir Percy’s character out of appearance, speech, action, and how other people react to him. A poet builds meaning a different way: out of the shape of the poem itself, how many lines it has, where the rhymes fall, and whether any line comes back.
Today you will read Chapters 3 and 4, then meet two named poem forms: the sonnet and the villanelle. You will see exactly how each one is built and notice how that structure shapes what the poem means.

A novelist builds a person out of detail after detail. A poet has a different set of tools. Two of the oldest and most exact are the sonnet and the villanelle. Both are named, fixed forms: a sonnet is always 14 lines, and a villanelle always repeats two of its own lines on a strict schedule. Once you can see the shape, you can see how the shape is doing work.
💡 THE SONNET: 14 LINES, THREE QUATRAINS AND A COUPLET
Quatrain 1
lines 1–4, rhymes A B A B
Quatrain 2
lines 5–8, rhymes C D C D
Quatrain 3
lines 9–12, rhymes E F E F
Closing couplet
lines 13–14, rhymes G G
Three quatrains build an idea, one step at a time. The couplet at the end is only two lines, but it lands with the most weight: it is where the poem turns or concludes.
📝 SONNET 18
William Shakespeare, first published 1609. Public domain.
A
Shall I compare thee to a summer’s day?
B
Thou art more lovely and more temperate:
A
Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,
B
And summer’s lease hath all too short a date:
C
Sometime too hot the eye of heaven shines,
D
And often is his gold complexion dimm’d,
C
And every fair from fair sometime declines,
D
By chance, or nature’s changing course untrimm’d:
E
But thy eternal summer shall not fade,
F
Nor lose possession of that fair thou ow’st,
E
Nor shall death brag thou wander’st in his shade,
F
When in eternal lines to time thou grow’st,
G
So long as men can breathe, or eyes can see,
G
So long lives this, and this gives life to thee.
⚡ READ & NOTICE: THE TURN IN THE COUPLET

Across the three quatrains, the speaker admits that summer fades, the sun dims, and beauty always declines eventually. Then the couplet changes direction: the poem itself, the speaker claims, will keep “thy eternal summer” alive as long as anyone reads it. The whole argument was built to arrive at those final two lines.

Read the sonnet aloud once more. What does the couplet do that the three quatrains alone could not? (No wrong answer.)
💡 THE VILLANELLE: 19 LINES THAT KEEP CIRCLING BACK

A villanelle is 19 lines: five three-line stanzas (tercets), then one four-line stanza (a quatrain) at the end. Two lines from the very first stanza return again and again on a fixed schedule, refrain A1 and refrain A2, until they finally stand side by side in the last two lines.

Tercet 1
lines 1–3: line 1 is refrain A1, line 3 is refrain A2
Tercet 2
lines 4–6: line 6 repeats refrain A1
Tercet 3
lines 7–9: line 9 repeats refrain A2
Tercet 4
lines 10–12: line 12 repeats refrain A1
Tercet 5
lines 13–15: line 15 repeats refrain A2
Closing quatrain
lines 16–19: line 18 repeats refrain A1, line 19 repeats refrain A2
By the last two lines, both refrains stand side by side. The poem does not move forward so much as it keeps returning to the same two thoughts.
📝 THE HOUSE ON THE HILL
Edwin Arlington Robinson, first published 1894. Public domain.
Refrain A1 is marked coral. Refrain A2 is marked teal.
They are all gone away,
The House is shut and still,
There is nothing more to say.
Through broken walls and gray
The winds blow bleak and shrill:
They are all gone away.
Nor is there one to-day
To speak them good or ill:
There is nothing more to say.
Why is it then we stray
Around that sunken sill?
They are all gone away,
And our poor fancy-play
For them is wasted skill:
There is nothing more to say.
There is ruin and decay
In the House on the Hill:
They are all gone away,
There is nothing more to say.
📚 READ & NOTICE: WHY THIS FORM, FOR THIS SUBJECT

The poem is about a house that everyone has left. Nothing happens in it: no plot, no new event, just the same two facts stated again and again, they are gone, and there is nothing more to say. A villanelle is the right form for exactly this situation. Its refrains do not let the poem move forward. Every time you expect new information, the poem circles back to what you already know. That refusal to advance is the whole feeling of standing outside a house that will never be lived in again.

💡 COMPARE THE TWO FORMS
The sonnet builds forward across fourteen lines to a couplet that resolves the argument. The villanelle circles the same two lines for nineteen lines and never fully resolves anything. Shakespeare needed a form that could argue and conclude. Robinson needed a form that could not move on. Each poet chose the shape that matched what the poem needed to do.

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A sonnet and a villanelle are both fixed shapes, built the same way every time they are used. That fixed shape is not a limit on the poet. It is a tool, chosen because it does one job better than any other form could. Later this week, you will choose one of these two forms yourself and build a poem inside it.
