Passive Voice: When the Action Lands on the Subject
Lesson ≈12 min
📝 TODAY'S FOCUS
In an active sentence, the subject does the action. In a passive sentence, the action is received by the subject, and the doer is hidden or pushed to the background. Orczy uses both, and understanding the difference will sharpen your reading and your writing.
📚 THIS WEEK'S SENTENCE (from Chapter 1)
“The guards at the gates had been doubled, the sergeants in command had been threatened with death.”
⚡ WARM-UP
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before the lesson, how confident are you that you can tell the difference between an active and a passive sentence? (No wrong answer.)
💡 THE ONE IDEA TO REMEMBER
Active voice
The subject does the action.
The committee threatened the sergeants.
Subject (the committee) acts.
Passive voice
The subject receives the action.
The sergeants had been threatened with death.
Subject (the sergeants) receives the action. The doer is not named.
The signal: a passive verb is almost always a form of to be (was, were, had been, is being) plus a past participle (doubled, threatened, sent). If you see that pattern, ask: who did this? If the answer is missing or tucked away after “by,” it is passive.
📚 HOW TO FIND PASSIVE VOICE: ASK WHO DID IT

Watch the questioning flow on this week’s sentence from Chapter 1.

  1. What is being said about the guards? They had been doubled. “Had been doubled” is a verb phrase: the helping verbs are had been and the main verb is doubled.
  2. Who or what doubled the guards? The sentence does not say. That missing doer is the mark of passive voice.
  3. Same test on the second clause: The sergeants had been threatened. Who threatened them? Again, not stated.
  4. Why does Orczy use passive here? The focus stays on what happened to the guards and sergeants, not on naming who gave the orders. The Revolutionary government looms in the background, unnamed.
Label what you found:
The guards
subject noun at the gates had been
helping verbs doubled
main verb (passive) .

Note: passive voice is not wrong. Writers choose it when the receiver of the action matters more than the doer, or when the doer is unknown or deliberately hidden. The challenge is knowing when to use it and when active voice is clearer.

✏️ APPLY IT IN YOUR WRITING

In your Reading Journal, write two sentences about something that happens in Chapter 1. Write the first sentence in active voice (the subject does the action). Then rewrite it in passive voice (the subject receives the action). Circle the verb in each.

Here is the structure to follow:

Active: The crowd cheered at the barricade.
Passive: The cheering was heard throughout the square. (Different subject, different focus.)
