How Orczy Builds Two Faces of One Character

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Chapter 6 gives you Percy’s biography. Every sentence about him sounds like mockery. Yet the last line of the chapter shows something that mockery cannot explain.
You will read Chapters 5 and 6, then study how Orczy uses the gap between what society sees and what the text shows as a craft technique. The goal is not to figure out who the Scarlet Pimpernel is, but to understand how a writer can hide a truth in plain sight.

💡 THE TECHNIQUE: WHAT SOCIETY SEES VS. WHAT THE TEXT SHOWS
Orczy spends most of Chapter 6 telling you what London society thinks of Percy. Then, at the very end, she gives you one moment that society did not see. That gap is the whole novel in miniature.
What society sees
  • “hopelessly stupid”
  • dull, lazy, inane laugh
  • married above himself
  • unaware his wife mocks him
  • can’t fight a duel
  • plays second fiddle at his own estate
What the text shows
  • Traveled widely before returning
  • Sir Andrew alone catches “intense longing, deep and hopeless passion”
  • Keeps perfect composure under the Vicomte’s provocation
  • Opens the door for Marguerite with “perfect, somewhat affected gallantry”
⚡ WARM-UP
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read Chapters 5 and 6: how convinced are you, so far, that Percy is really as dim as everyone says? (No wrong answer.)
📚 PART 1 · READ & NOTICE: READ CHAPTERS 5 AND 6

In Chapter 5, Lady Blakeney sweeps into the coffee-room. The Comtesse refuses to let her daughter greet Marguerite. Marguerite covers the snub with wit and mimicry. Percy then arrives. In Chapter 6, Orczy stops the story to give you Percy’s full biography: his upbringing between an imbecile mother and a distracted father, his wealth and title, why London society regards him as an idiot, and why Marguerite seems to treat her marriage as a joke. The chapter ends at the coffee-room door, just before Armand St. Just boards the Daydream for France. And Sir Andrew, looking back, catches something in Percy’s face.

💡 KEEP THIS IN MIND AS YOU READ
Chapter 6 is entirely the narrator’s summary of what “society” believes. Ask: is the narrator endorsing society’s opinion, or reporting it? There is a difference. Watch for places where the narrator’s word choice seems to lean slightly away from the crowd.
⚡ TRY IT · WHICH COLUMN DOES IT BELONG TO?

Each detail below comes from Chapters 5 or 6. Does it belong to what society sees, or to what the text quietly shows underneath that surface? Decide before you click.

1. “Sir Percy had a British accent you could cut with a knife,” says Marguerite in Ch. 6.
2. “...a curious look of intense longing, of deep and hopeless passion, with which the inane and flippant Sir Percy followed the retreating figure of his brilliant wife.” (Ch. 6)
3. “London society thought that...it would have been wiser on his part had he bestowed those worldly advantages upon a less brilliant and witty wife.” (Ch. 6)
4. “With that perfect, somewhat affected gallantry which characterised his every movement, he opened the coffee-room door for her.” (Ch. 6)
📚 PART 3 · READ & NOTICE: YOUR READING JOURNAL
✏️ READING JOURNAL · ADD THIS TO THE WEEK 2 SECTION

Answer each question in a few sentences.

  1. In Chapter 5, Marguerite faces a public insult with composure, then mimics the Comtesse almost the moment she leaves. What does Orczy show about Marguerite’s character through those two moves in a row?
  2. Chapter 6 is written from London society’s point of view. Find one place where the narrator seems to step slightly outside that point of view and let you see something society misses. Quote the phrase and explain what it suggests.
  3. By the end of Chapter 6, do you think the “inane laugh” is real or performed? Use at least one detail from the text to support your answer. There is no single right answer here.

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A disguise only works if the person wearing it is completely convincing. For the disguise to convince London society for years, the performance has to be total. That kind of self-control is not stupidity. It is something else entirely.
