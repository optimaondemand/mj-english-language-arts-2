Lesson 5.03 · Weekly Workshop · Week 5
Weekly Workshop: The Claim About the Climax
Lesson ≈10 min
📝 TODAY'S FOCUS
Today you will prepare to write Quick Write 5, your project feeder. You have a choice: write a claim about which moment in Chapters 9 and 10 is the true climax, OR write a counterclaim to your existing argument about the novel's themes. This workshop helps you decide and gives you what you need to get started.
💡 THE CONCEPT AT A GLANCE
A CLAIM
States what you believe is true and why, using evidence.
You take a position. You name a specific moment, idea, or pattern in the text, and you give a reason backed by something that happens in the story. A claim puts a stake in the ground.
EXAMPLE
In the novel, the true turning point is not the battle, but the quiet moment of decision afterward.
A COUNTERCLAIM
Names a view that disagrees with your claim, then explains why your claim is still stronger.
A counterclaim does not abandon your argument. It honestly states the other side, shows you understand it, and then explains why your reasoning holds up anyway. This is what makes an argument persuasive rather than one-sided.
EXAMPLE
Some readers argue that the battle itself is the climax, since it is the highest point of physical action. However, the true turning point comes after...
📖 PART 1 · READ & NOTICE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · NO WRONG ANSWER
Which do you think is harder to write: making a strong claim, or honestly addressing a counterclaim?
📖 PART 2 · READ & NOTICE

In a literary-analysis argument, a claim names a position about the text and gives a reason grounded in what actually happens in the story. A counterclaim takes that argument one step further: it honestly names the strongest opposing view, acknowledges why someone might believe it, and then explains why the original claim holds up anyway. Consider this pair of sentences, written about a fictional novel, not about The Outsiders:

Claim:
In the novel, the true turning point is not the battle, but the quiet moment of decision afterward.
Counterclaim:
Some readers argue that the battle itself is the climax, since it is the highest point of physical action. However, action alone does not make a turning point: what makes it a turning point is that someone changes because of it, and that change happens in the quiet that follows.

The counterclaim does not give up the argument. It names the other side, shows that it deserves to be taken seriously, and then explains why the original claim is still stronger. That combination is what makes literary argument more than opinion: it is intellectual honesty in writing.

🆕 PART 3 · MAKE & APPLY

Before you open Quick Write 5, decide in your notebook which option you will write. Then click the button to see a tip for getting started.

OPTION A: Write a Claim
Name the moment in Chapters 9 or 10 you believe is the true climax of the novel and explain why, with at least one reason tied to what changes in the story.
Tip for Option A
A strong claim names the specific moment (not just "the end of the chapter") and gives one reason tied to what shifts because of it: what does a character understand differently, decide, or lose after this moment? That change is your reason. Start by finishing this sentence in your notebook: "The true climax in Chapters 9 and 10 is [moment] because..."
OPTION B: Write a Counterclaim
Take your earlier argument about a theme in the novel and write a counterclaim: name the opposing view honestly, explain why someone could believe it, then explain why your original argument is still stronger.
Tip for Option B
A strong counterclaim names the opposing view at its best, not at its worst. If you argue for one side, find the most reasonable thing someone on the other side could say, state it fairly, and then explain what your argument has that theirs does not. Start by writing: "Some readers might argue that... However, this view does not account for..."
