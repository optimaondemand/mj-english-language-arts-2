Figurative Language and Voice
Lesson ≈13 min
📖 FOCUS
A writer’s voice is not only what is said but how it is said.
When a narrator reaches for a comparison or an unexpected image, they are revealing how they see the world. This lesson names those moves and practices spotting them in original sentences before locating them in the reading.

2
I will be able to explain what figurative language does that plain description cannot.

💡 THE THREE TOOLS TO KNOW
Simile
Compares two things using like or as.
“The crowd was as restless as a river after a storm.”
Metaphor
Compares two things WITHOUT like or as.
“The afternoon was a slow burn of boredom.”
Personification
Gives human qualities to a non-human thing.
“The old car coughed and refused to start.”
All three are ways a writer reaches for something more vivid, more precise, and more honest than a plain description can be.

📚 PART 2 · READ & NOTICE: WHAT FIGURATIVE LANGUAGE DOES

When writers want to describe something vividly, they often compare it to something else. A simile uses the words like or as to make the comparison direct. A metaphor drops those words and states the comparison as if it were simply true. Personification gives human qualities to something that is not human, making it feel alive and purposeful. A narrator who reaches for these comparisons naturally is revealing how they think and what they notice. The specific things a character compares reveal as much about the narrator as about whatever is being described.

Consider: What is the difference between saying “the sun was hot” and “the sun hammered down on us”?
The second version uses a metaphor (hammered), which makes the sun sound like a force attacking. That comparison shows that the narrator does not just notice heat; the narrator feels it as an assault. That is the work figurative language does: it shows not just what something is, but how it feels from the inside.
