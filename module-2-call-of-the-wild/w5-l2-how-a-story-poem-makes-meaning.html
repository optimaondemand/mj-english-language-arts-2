Lesson 5.02
How a Story-Poem Makes Meaning
Lesson · pairing a poem with the world of the novel
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
⌚ ~14 min
London makes the frozen Northland feel real in prose. A poet can make the same wild country feel alive in a completely different way: through the sound and shape of the words themselves.
You will read a short poem that tells a story set in the same frozen North as Buck’s world. A poem like this is built to be heard. You will study four tools the poet uses, beat, rhyme, a repeated stanza that frames the whole poem, and storytelling, and notice how each one changes the way the poem feels.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name four tools a story-poem uses: a steady beat, end rhyme, a repeated stanza that frames the poem, and a clear plot.
2
I will be able to explain how the beat and the returning frame stanza change the tone of the poem.
3
I will be able to compare how the poem and the novel each bring the Northland to life.
💡 THE ONE IDEA TO HOLD ONTO
A story told in a poem uses four tools to shape how it feels. Each tool does a job. Learn the job, not just the name.
🥁
A steady beat (rhythm)
The lines have a swinging, marching pulse you can almost tap. It carries you forward, like a steady stride across the snow.
🔗
End rhyme
The line endings chime together. Rhyme ties lines into pairs and makes the poem easy to remember and fun to say aloud.
🔄
A repeated stanza (the frame)
A whole stanza opens the poem and then comes back to close it. It works like a frame around a picture: you hear it, you travel through the story, and you land on it again changed.
📜
A real plot
The poem tells a full story with characters and a beginning, middle, and end. It is a tale, set to a beat.
📚 READ & NOTICE: THE POEM

This week’s poem is The Cremation of Sam McGee by Robert Service, first published in 1907 in his collection Songs of a Sourdough. It tells the strange, darkly funny story of two prospectors in the frozen Yukon: the narrator and his friend Sam McGee, who dreads the cold so much that he asks, when he dies, to be cremated rather than left in the ice. The poem is in the public domain.

📝 THE CREMATION OF SAM McGEE
Robert W. Service, from Songs of a Sourdough (1907). Public domain.
There are strange things done in the midnight sun
By the men who moil for gold;
The Arctic trails have their secret tales
That would make your blood run cold;
The Northern Lights have seen queer sights,
But the queerest they ever did see
Was that night on the marge of Lake Lebarge
I cremated Sam McGee.
Now Sam McGee was from Tennessee, where the cotton blooms and blows.
Why he left his home in the South to roam round the Pole God only knows.
He was always cold, but the land of gold seemed to hold him like a spell;
Though he'd often say in his homely way that he'd "sooner live in hell."
On a Christmas Day we were mushing our way over the Dawson trail.
Talk of your cold! through the parka's fold it stabbed like a driven nail.
If our eyes we'd close, then the lashes froze, till sometimes we couldn't see;
It wasn't much fun, but the only one to whimper was Sam McGee.
And that very night as we lay packed tight in our robes beneath the snow,
And the dogs were fed, and the stars o'erhead were dancing heel and toe,
He turned to me, and, "Cap," says he, "I'll cash in this trip, I guess;
And if I do, I'm asking that you won't refuse my last request."
Well, he seemed so low that I couldn't say no: then he says with a sort of moan:
"It's the cursed cold, and it's got right hold till I'm chilled clean through to the bone.
Yet 'taint being dead, it's my awful dread of the icy grave that pains:
So I want you to swear that, foul or fair, you'll cremate my last remains."
A pal's last need is a thing to heed, so I swore I would not fail;
And we started on at the streak of dawn, but God! he looked ghastly pale.
He crouched on the sleigh, and he raved all day of his home in Tennessee;
And before nightfall a corpse was all that was left of Sam McGee.
There wasn't a breath in that land of death, and I hurried, horror driven,
With a corpse half-hid that I couldn't get rid because of a promise given;
It was lashed to the sleigh, and it seemed to say: "You may tax your brawn and brains,
But you promised true, and it's up to you to cremate those last remains."
Now a promise made is a debt unpaid, and the trail has its own stern code.
In the days to come, though my lips were dumb, in my heart how I cursed that load.
In the long, long night, by the lone firelight, while the huskies, round in a ring,
Howled out their woes to the homeless snows--O God! how I loathed the thing!
And every day that quiet clay seemed to heavy and heavier grow;
And on I went, though the dogs were spent and the grub was getting low;
The trail was bad, and I felt half mad, but I swore I would not give in;
And I'd often sing to the hateful thing, and it hearkened with a grin.
Till I came to the marge of Lake Lebarge, and a derelict there lay;
It was jammed in the ice, but I saw in a trice it was called the "Alice May."
And I looked at it, and I thought a bit, and I looked at my frozen chum:
Then, "Here," said I, with a sudden cry, "is my cre-ma-tor-eum."
Some planks I tore from the cabin floor, and I lit the boiler fire;
Some coal I found that was lying around, and I heaped the fuel higher;
The flames just soared, and the furnace roared--such a blaze you seldom see;
And I burrowed a hole in the glowing coal, and I stuffed in Sam McGee.
Then I made a hike, for I didn't like to hear him sizzle so;
And the heavens scowled, and the huskies howled, and the wind began to blow.
It was icy cold, but the hot sweat rolled down my cheeks, and I don't know why;
And the greasy smoke in an inky cloak went streaking down the sky.
I do not know how long in the snow I wrestled with grisly fear;
But the stars came out and they danced about ere again I ventured near;
I was sick with dread, but I bravely said: "I'll just take a peep inside.
I guess he's cooked, and it's time I looked," ... then the door I opened wide.
And there sat Sam, looking cool and calm, in the heart of the furnace roar;
And he wore a smile you could see a mile, and he said: "Please close that door.
It's fine in here, but I greatly fear you'll let in the cold and storm--
Since I left Plumtree, down in Tennessee, it's the first time I've been warm."
There are strange things done in the midnight sun
By the men who moil for gold;
The Arctic trails have their secret tales
That would make your blood run cold;
The Northern Lights have seen queer sights,
But the queerest they ever did see
Was that night on the marge of Lake Lebarge
I cremated Sam McGee.
The gold-bordered stanza is the one to watch. Notice where it appears.
💡 HOW TO READ IT
Read the poem aloud, even quietly to yourself. This kind of poem is built for the ear. Tap the beat with your finger as you go. The poem opens with a stanza in a gold border. When you reach the end, watch for it coming back. Notice whether it feels the same the second time.

📚 READ & NOTICE: HOW THE FORM SHAPES THE TONE

The story is dark: a man freezes to death in the Arctic and his friend burns the body. Yet the poem does not feel like a tragedy. The bouncy beat and the chiming rhymes give it a wild, almost grinning energy. The poet matched a light, sing-song form to a grim story on purpose, and that mismatch is exactly what makes the poem feel eerie and funny at the same time.

That is the heart of today’s skill: a poem’s form, its beat, its rhyme, its returning frame, is not just decoration. It shapes the tone, the feeling the poem gives off. Change the form and you change how the same events feel.

💡 KEEP THIS IN MIND
If this story were written as flat, plain sentences with no beat and no rhyme, it would read like a grim news report. The form is what turns it into something you want to say out loud by the fire.
⚡ TRY IT · WHICH TOOL IS DOING THE WORK?

Each statement describes something you noticed in the poem. Choose the tool that explains it. Decide in your head before you choose.

1. The eight lines that open the poem show up again, word for word, at the very end.
2. The word at the end of one line chimes with the word ending the next.
3. You can feel a swinging pulse in the lines that you could tap a foot to.
4. The poem follows two men through a problem from start to finish.
📚 READ & NOTICE: THE POEM AND THE NOVEL

Both this poem and The Call of the Wild are set in the same frozen North, full of cold, danger, and gold-seekers. But they bring that world to life in different ways. London uses long, careful prose to put you inside Buck’s mind. The poem uses beat, rhyme, and a stanza that frames the whole tale to make the Northland feel like a wild song.

✏️ READING JOURNAL · ADD THIS TO THE WEEK 5 SECTION

Answer in a few sentences each. Point to the poem for the first two.

  1. The poem opens and closes with the same stanza. Read it once at the start and once at the end. Does it feel different the second time? Why would a poet frame a story this way instead of just stopping when the story stops?
  2. The story is grim, but the poem feels lively, even funny. Name one tool of the poem’s form (its beat, rhyme, or returning frame) and explain how it creates that mismatch in tone.
  3. Both the poem and the novel show the frozen North. Name one thing the poem’s form lets it do that London’s prose does not, and one thing London’s prose does that the poem cannot.
