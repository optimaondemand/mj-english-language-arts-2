📚 PART 1 · READ & NOTICE: THE REAL KLONDIKE

Read this informational text about the world London is writing into. Notice how it is organized.

Freight, Furs, and Forty Below: Supplying the Klondike Gold Rush
GETTING THERE

In 1897, tens of thousands of people rushed toward the Klondike goldfields in Canada’s Yukon Territory, most traveling through the port towns of Skagway and Dyea in Alaska. From there, the only way north was on foot, over mountain passes like the Chilkoot, carrying everything they would need. Canadian officials at the border required each person to bring about a ton of food and gear, roughly enough to last a year, so that the rush would not turn into a famine. Almost no one could carry a ton in one trip, so people climbed the pass again and again, ferrying their supplies load by load.

WHAT THEY CARRIED

A typical outfit was heavy on food that would not spoil: bacon, beans, flour, dried fruit, and hardtack, a hard biscuit that lasts almost indefinitely. Clothing had to survive temperatures far below zero: heavy wool layers, fur coats and hats, and mukluks, soft boots suited to deep snow. None of it was decorative. Every item had to earn its place in the pack.

MOVING FREIGHT

Once supplies reached the interior, sled dogs did much of the heavy work, hauling freight, mail, and passengers over frozen rivers and trails where no other transportation could get through. A strong team of dogs could move loads that would be impossible for a person on foot alone, which is exactly the kind of work Buck is bred and beaten into doing.

DAWSON CITY

By 1898, Dawson City, at the meeting of the Klondike and Yukon Rivers, had grown from a small camp into a boomtown of thousands, seemingly overnight. Much of the Yukon’s interior was still being mapped as prospectors arrived; surveyors and explorers were still filling in the details of a region that, only a few years earlier, had barely appeared on any map at all.

📚 PART 2 · FINDING A SOURCE OF YOUR OWN
💭 WARM-UP · NO WRONG ANSWER
You want to check whether something you read online about history is actually true. What do you look at first?

JStor is a large digital library of articles that other experts have checked before publishing. Getting the most out of it takes two steps:

STEP 1 · JSTOR DAILY (daily.jstor.org)
Free, no login. Short, readable articles, and every one links to the scholarship behind it. Start here to find your angle.
STEP 2 · JSTOR (jstor.org)
Follow the link into the full article, or search directly with two or three key words.
🔑 GETTING IN, AND WHERE TO SEE THE FULL SOURCE BANK
The JStor login is shared and, for security, is not printed on any GitHub-hosted page. Open the Research and JStor Access page in Canvas to sign in. For a fuller list of free, reputable places to search beyond JStor, and for search-term ideas about this world, open the Research Starting Points reference page. Bookmark both.
⚖️ PART 3 · JUDGE WHAT YOU FIND

You already know how to test a character’s argument from the How Arguments Work reference page. Testing a source you found uses the same habit of mind:

  1. Who wrote it? A scholar, a journalist, a company selling something?
  2. What does it claim? State the main point in one sentence, in your own words.
  3. What does it rest on? Primary documents and named experts, or nothing you can check?
  4. Who was it written for? A general reader, other scholars, a customer?
  5. What does it leave out? Name one thing a fuller account would need to mention.

Want more practice telling a strong source from a weak one? The Research Starting Points reference page has two more examples to try.

✏️ READING JOURNAL · ADD THIS TO THE WEEK 4 SECTION

For your one or two sources, record:

  1. The topic you chose, and the title and site of each source.
  2. One fact or claim each source makes, in your own words.
  3. A one-line evaluation of one source: who wrote it, and what it rests on.

This is a light, exploratory search, not a research paper. One or two sources is enough. Your teacher reviews the Journal at the module checkpoint, not this task on its own.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND SOURCES
An AI tool can help you turn your topic into search words. It cannot tell you what sources actually exist, and it sometimes invents titles and authors that sound real but are not. Only record a source you found and opened yourself.
