Oberon and Titania Quarrel
Lesson
⏱ ~12 min
TODAY’S FOCUS
In Week 1 you watched Egeus narrate Lysander’s courtship as theft, to win a ruling from the duke. Today the fairy queen Titania tells two stories of her own: one about a boy’s mother, and one about the weather. Both stories are meant to persuade. Your job is to notice what each one is doing.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify what a character wants when they tell a particular version of events.
2
I will be able to tell the difference between something the play shows as literally true and a character’s persuasive account of events.
3
I will be able to compare Titania’s narrations with Egeus’s narration from Week 1.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED
Act 2, Scene 1 (embedded below) and your Reading Journal.
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
When someone tells you a story to explain why they made a choice, do you usually take it at face value, or do you wonder what they want you to think? (No wrong answer.)
👁 READ & NOTICE · NARRATION 1
THE STORY OF THE BOY’S MOTHER

Oberon wants Titania to give him her young attendant, a changeling boy. Instead of simply refusing, Titania tells a story.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 2, SCENE 1
His mother was a vot’ress of my order,
And in the spicèd Indian air, by night,
Full often hath she gossip’d by my side;
And sat with me on Neptune’s yellow sands,
Marking th’ embarkèd traders on the flood,
When we have laugh’d to see the sails conceive,
And grow big-bellied with the wanton wind;
Which she, with pretty and with swimming gait
Following (her womb then rich with my young squire),
Would imitate, and sail upon the land,
To fetch me trifles, and return again,
As from a voyage, rich with merchandise.
But she, being mortal, of that boy did die;
And for her sake do I rear up her boy,
And for her sake I will not part with him.
Titania, refusing to give up the changeling boy. Act 2, Scene 1.

Titania describes a friendship with the boy’s mother: laughing together, watching ships, the mother imitating their swelling sails while pregnant. It is a tender, specific memory. It is also exactly the kind of story that makes it much harder for Oberon, or the audience, to simply say “hand the child over.”

👁 READ & NOTICE · NARRATION 2
THE STORY OF THE DISORDERED SEASONS

Titania also blames the couple’s quarrel for something much larger: floods, rot in the fields, a winter with no proper cold, seasons that no longer know which is which.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 2, SCENE 1
These are the forgeries of jealousy:
And never, since the middle summer’s spring,
Met we on hill, in dale, forest, or mead,
By pavèd fountain, or by rushy brook,
Or on the beachèd margent of the sea,
To dance our ringlets to the whistling wind,
But with thy brawls thou hast disturb’d our sport.
Therefore the winds, piping to us in vain,
As in revenge, have suck’d up from the sea
Contagious fogs; which, falling in the land,
Hath every pelting river made so proud
That they have overborne their continents.
The ox hath therefore stretch’d his yoke in vain,
The ploughman lost his sweat, and the green corn
Hath rotted ere his youth attain’d a beard.
The fold stands empty in the drownèd field,
And crows are fatted with the murrion flock;
The nine-men’s-morris is fill’d up with mud,
And the quaint mazes in the wanton green,
For lack of tread, are undistinguishable.
The human mortals want their winter here.
No night is now with hymn or carol blest.
Therefore the moon, the governess of floods,
Pale in her anger, washes all the air,
That rheumatic diseases do abound.
And thorough this distemperature we see
The seasons alter: hoary-headed frosts
Fall in the fresh lap of the crimson rose;
And on old Hiems’ thin and icy crown
An odorous chaplet of sweet summer buds
Is, as in mockery, set. The spring, the summer,
The childing autumn, angry winter, change
Their wonted liveries; and the mazed world,
By their increase, now knows not which is which.
And this same progeny of evils comes
From our debate, from our dissension;
We are their parents and original.
Titania, on the disordered seasons. Act 2, Scene 1.
🧠 THINK ABOUT IT
Is Titania’s claim a cause, or a story about cause? In this play’s own world, fairies really do have power over nature, so her claim could be literally true. But notice what the claim also does: it makes the quarrel sound enormous and blames Oberon for real damage in the human world. A true thing and a persuasive thing are not always different things.
📝 PRACTICE
WHY TELL THIS STORY?
For each narration, choose the best explanation of what the story accomplishes for the character who tells it.
1. Why does Titania tell the story of the boy’s mother rather than simply refusing Oberon?
2. What does the weather narration accomplish for Titania in her argument with Oberon?
3. What does Titania’s storytelling have in common with Egeus’s speech to Theseus in Week 1?

In the Week 2 section of your Reading Journal, compare Egeus’s narration (Week 1) with one of Titania’s narrations from today. What does each character want, and how does the story they tell help them get it? Write three to four sentences. Add one line to your evolving answer to the module question: whose account can we trust so far?
