Why Does Everyone Tell a Story?

📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
⌚ ~18 min
All module, you have watched people inside this play tell stories: to a duke, to each other, to themselves. Today you look at all of them side by side. Then you return to the question that has run under the whole module: whose account can we trust, and how would we know?
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name several characters who narrate an account of events inside the play.
2
I will be able to compare what each narrator wants a listener to believe with what the audience actually saw happen.
3
I will be able to begin to form my own answer to the module question, using evidence from more than one narrator.
❓ THE MODULE QUESTION
Everyone in this play tells a story about what happened in the wood. Whose account can we trust, and how would we know?
This lesson will not answer that question for you. Keep it in mind as you read each account below.
📚 THE STORYTELLERS, ACT BY ACT
Seven moments where a character inside the play tells someone a story about what happened.
📜 EGEUS TO THESEUS · ACT 1

At the very start of the play, Egeus stands in front of the Duke and accuses Lysander of stealing his daughter’s love.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 1, SCENE 1
This man hath bewitch’d the bosom of my child.
Thou, thou, Lysander, thou hast given her rhymes,
And interchang’d love-tokens with my child.
Thou hast by moonlight at her window sung,
With feigning voice, verses of feigning love;
Egeus, in Act 1, Scene 1, telling Theseus his version of Lysander’s courtship.
Notice: Egeus calls the courtship bewitching and theft. What might Lysander call the very same events?
📜 TITANIA ON THE BOY’S MOTHER · ACT 2

When Oberon demands the changeling boy, Titania answers with a memory of the boy’s mother, a friend of hers who has died.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 2, SCENE 1
His mother was a vot’ress of my order,
And in the spicèd Indian air, by night,
Full often hath she gossip’d by my side;
Titania, in Act 2, Scene 1, remembering the boy’s mother.
Notice: This memory makes keeping the boy sound like loyalty, not stubbornness. Does the play show you enough of the mother to check the account?
📜 TITANIA ON THE SEASONS · ACT 2

In the same argument, Titania claims that her quarrel with Oberon has broken the weather itself.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 2, SCENE 1
And this same progeny of evils comes
From our debate, from our dissension;
We are their parents and original.
Titania, in the same argument, naming the cause of the broken seasons.
Notice: Titania states this as plain fact. Is there any way for the audience to test whether a fairy quarrel really controls the seasons?
📜 HELENA’S CHILDHOOD · ACT 3

When Hermia turns on her, Helena reaches for the one thing she has always trusted: their shared past.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 3, SCENE 2
Like to a double cherry, seeming parted,
But yet a union in partition,
Two lovely berries moulded on one stem;
Helena, in Act 3, Scene 2, reminding Hermia of their childhood.
Notice: Helena is not just remembering. She is using the memory to win an argument happening right now. Does that change how you read it?
📜 BOTTOM’S DREAM · ACT 4

Bottom wakes from his night as a donkey loved by a fairy queen and tries, and fails, to describe it.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 4, SCENE 1
I have had a most rare vision. I have had a dream, past the wit
of man to say what dream it was. Man is but an ass if he go about to
expound this dream.
Bottom, in Act 4, Scene 1, waking from his night with Titania.
📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 4, SCENE 1
The eye of man hath not
heard, the ear of man hath not seen, man’s hand is not able to taste,
his tongue to conceive, nor his heart to report, what my dream was.
Bottom, moments later, still trying and failing to report it.
Notice: Bottom says no one can report what he saw, then plans to turn it into a ballad anyway. What does that tell you about how stories get told, even when the teller admits they cannot really tell them?
📜 THESEUS AND HIPPOLYTA DISAGREE · ACT 5

The morning after, the lovers describe a night in the wood that Theseus was not there to see. He and Hippolyta argue about whether to believe it.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 5, SCENE 1
The lunatic, the lover, and the poet
Are of imagination all compact:
Theseus, in Act 5, Scene 1, dismissing the lovers’ account before he hears the details.
📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 5, SCENE 1
But all the story of the night told over,
And all their minds transfigur’d so together,
More witnesseth than fancy’s images,
And grows to something of great constancy;
Hippolyta, answering him. Four people telling the same strange story is itself a kind of evidence.
Notice: Theseus and Hippolyta heard the exact same story. Why might two reasonable people disagree about whether to believe it?
📜 THE MECHANICALS TELL IT BADLY · ACT 5

That evening, a group of workmen perform the story of Pyramus and Thisbe for the court, and the performance falls apart.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 5, SCENE 1
This is the silliest stuff that ever I heard.

THESEUS.
The best in this kind are but shadows; and the worst are no worse, if
imagination amend them.
Hippolyta and Theseus, watching the mechanicals’ performance of Pyramus and Thisbe fall apart.
Notice: The mechanicals are trying to tell a true, tragic love story. Why does the telling turn it into a comedy instead?
📜 ONE STORYTELLER LEFT

In the last lesson, you met an eighth storyteller: Puck, who stepped fully outside the play to speak to you, the audience, directly. Seven characters, and a play itself, all telling a story about the same strange night in seven very different ways.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DID EACH ONE WANT YOU TO BELIEVE?
1. Egeus describes Lysander’s courtship of Hermia as…
2. Hippolyta argues that the lovers’ matching stories are…
3. Bottom claims that his dream is…
4. The mechanicals’ performance turns their planned tragedy into…
✏️ APPLY IT: READING JOURNAL, SECTION 1
✏️ READING JOURNAL · SECTION 1, WEEK 6

Pick two of the seven narrators from the gallery above. For each one, write one sentence on what they wanted their listener to believe, and one sentence on whether you find their account trustworthy, and why.

Then write two or three sentences: what is your current, evolving answer to the module question? You do not need a final answer. You need a reasoned one, built on evidence from the play.

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A play cannot tell you which account to believe. It can only show you people telling accounts, carefully, one after another, and let you watch how each one is built.
