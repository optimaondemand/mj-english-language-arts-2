Lesson 2.03
The Love-Juice and the First Mistake

TODAY’S FOCUS
Oberon sends Puck for a magic flower and explains where its power comes from. Then Puck goes looking for an unkind Athenian man to enchant, finds a sleeping Athenian man, and gets it wrong. Today you will read the setup and the mistake, and learn the term for what happens when the audience knows something a character does not.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to summarize the myth Oberon tells about how the flower gained its power, including its allusion to Cupid.
2
I will be able to explain how the wood’s darkness leads directly to Puck’s mistake.
3
I will be able to define dramatic irony and identify it in this scene.

👁 READ & NOTICE · THE FLOWER’S ORIGIN
OBERON’S STORY TO PUCK
📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 2, SCENE 1
That very time I saw, (but thou couldst not),
Flying between the cold moon and the earth,
Cupid all arm’d: a certain aim he took
At a fair vestal, thronèd by the west,
And loos’d his love-shaft smartly from his bow
As it should pierce a hundred thousand hearts.
But I might see young Cupid’s fiery shaft
Quench’d in the chaste beams of the watery moon;
And the imperial votress passed on,
In maiden meditation, fancy-free.
Yet mark’d I where the bolt of Cupid fell:
It fell upon a little western flower,
Before milk-white, now purple with love’s wound,
And maidens call it love-in-idleness.
Fetch me that flower, the herb I showed thee once:
The juice of it on sleeping eyelids laid
Will make or man or woman madly dote
Upon the next live creature that it sees.
Fetch me this herb, and be thou here again
Ere the leviathan can swim a league.
Oberon tells Puck the origin of the flower’s power. Act 2, Scene 1.
🌅 ALLUSION
Oberon’s story is a classical allusion: it names Cupid, the Roman god of love, who is imagined shooting a “love-shaft” from his bow at a passing maiden. The arrow misses her and strikes a flower instead, and that is how the flower gains the power to make anyone fall in love with the next living creature they see. Shakespeare’s audience would have recognized Cupid immediately. Naming him roots this fairy-tale explanation in a story his audience already knew.
👁 READ & NOTICE · THE MISTAKE
PUCK FINDS THE WRONG MAN

Oberon told Puck to find a young Athenian man being unkind to a woman who loves him, and to put the juice on his eyes. Puck goes looking in the dark.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 2, SCENE 2
Through the forest have I gone,
But Athenian found I none,
On whose eyes I might approve
This flower’s force in stirring love.
Night and silence! Who is here?
Weeds of Athens he doth wear:
This is he, my master said,
Despisèd the Athenian maid;
And here the maiden, sleeping sound,
On the dank and dirty ground.
Pretty soul, she durst not lie
Near this lack-love, this kill-courtesy.
Churl, upon thy eyes I throw
All the power this charm doth owe;
When thou wak’st let love forbid
Sleep his seat on thy eyelid.
So awake when I am gone;
For I must now to Oberon.
Puck finds a sleeping Athenian man and acts on Oberon’s order. Act 2, Scene 2.

Puck sees a sleeping man in Athenian clothes and a sleeping woman nearby, and assumes this must be the pair Oberon described. It is not. This is Lysander and Hermia, two lovers who are devoted to each other, not the unkind Demetrius and the rejected Helena. In full daylight, with a clear view, this mix-up would be far less likely. In the dark wood, it happens instantly.

👁 READ & NOTICE · THE RESULT
LYSANDER WAKES

Helena, still chasing Demetrius, stumbles onto the sleeping Lysander and wakes him. The juice does its work immediately.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 2, SCENE 2
And run through fire I will for thy sweet sake.
Transparent Helena! Nature shows art,
That through thy bosom makes me see thy heart.
Where is Demetrius? O, how fit a word
Is that vile name to perish on my sword!

HELENA.
Do not say so, Lysander, say not so.
What though he love your Hermia? Lord, what though?
Yet Hermia still loves you. Then be content.

LYSANDER.
Content with Hermia? No, I do repent
The tedious minutes I with her have spent.
Not Hermia, but Helena I love.
Who will not change a raven for a dove?
The will of man is by his reason sway’d,
And reason says you are the worthier maid.
Things growing are not ripe until their season;
So I, being young, till now ripe not to reason;
And touching now the point of human skill,
Reason becomes the marshal to my will,
And leads me to your eyes, where I o’erlook
Love’s stories, written in love’s richest book.
Lysander wakes and declares love for Helena instead of Hermia. Act 2, Scene 2.
👀 DRAMATIC IRONY
Dramatic irony happens when the audience knows something an onstage character does not. Here, you know exactly why Lysander suddenly loves Helena: Puck used the flower on the wrong man. Helena and Lysander do not know that. Helena, hearing a man she thought loved someone else suddenly declare love for her, assumes she is being mocked. You can feel the gap between what you know and what they know.

CAUSE, EFFECT, AND IRONY

2. Why is Lysander’s sudden declaration of love for Helena an example of dramatic irony?
