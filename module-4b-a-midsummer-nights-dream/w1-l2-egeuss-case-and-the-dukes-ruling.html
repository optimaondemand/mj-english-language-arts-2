Lesson 1.02
Egeus’s Case and the Duke’s Ruling
Lesson
⏱ ~15 min
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
What happens when someone tells a story about you to get what they want from someone in charge?
In the play’s opening scene, Egeus marches his daughter Hermia in front of Duke Theseus and makes a formal complaint. Today you will pull that complaint apart the way a careful reader should: find the claim he is making, the evidence he offers for it, and the audience he is making it to. Then you will watch what the audience actually sees a few lines later, and ask whether Egeus’s account was the whole truth.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify the claim, the evidence, and the intended audience in a speech from the play.
2
I will be able to state exactly what ruling Egeus demands from the Duke.
3
I will be able to compare Egeus’s account of the courtship with what the audience witnesses directly, a few lines later, with its own eyes.
💡 FOUR PARTS OF A FORMAL COMPLAINT
Claim
What the speaker wants you to believe is true.
Evidence
The specific details the speaker offers to back up the claim.
Audience
The specific person or people the speaker is trying to persuade.
Demand
The specific ruling or action the speaker wants the audience to grant.

📚 PART 2 · READ & NOTICE: EGEUS MAKES HIS CASE

Egeus arrives at the Duke’s palace with his daughter Hermia, along with Lysander (the man Hermia loves) and Demetrius (the man Egeus has chosen for her). Read his full complaint below.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 1, SCENE 1
Full of vexation come I, with complaint
Against my child, my daughter Hermia.
Stand forth, Demetrius. My noble lord,
This man hath my consent to marry her.
Stand forth, Lysander. And, my gracious Duke,
This man hath bewitch’d the bosom of my child.
Thou, thou, Lysander, thou hast given her rhymes,
And interchang’d love-tokens with my child.
Thou hast by moonlight at her window sung,
With feigning voice, verses of feigning love;
And stol’n the impression of her fantasy
With bracelets of thy hair, rings, gauds, conceits,
Knacks, trifles, nosegays, sweetmeats (messengers
Of strong prevailment in unharden’d youth)
With cunning hast thou filch’d my daughter’s heart,
Turn’d her obedience (which is due to me)
To stubborn harshness. And, my gracious Duke,
Be it so she will not here before your grace
Consent to marry with Demetrius,
I beg the ancient privilege of Athens:
As she is mine I may dispose of her;
Which shall be either to this gentleman
Or to her death, according to our law
Egeus to Duke Theseus (Act 1, Scene 1)
💡 TAKING THE SPEECH APART
Claim
“As she is mine I may dispose of her;” Egeus claims that, as her father, he has the right to decide who Hermia marries, and that her own wishes do not need to be part of the decision.
Evidence
Egeus lists exactly what Lysander did: gave Hermia rhymes, traded love-tokens with her, sang under her window by moonlight, and gave her bracelets made of his hair, rings, and small gifts. He calls these “messengers of strong prevailment in unharden’d youth,” meaning tools that work especially well on someone young and inexperienced. Notice that Egeus describes ordinary courtship gifts as if they were tools of manipulation.
Audience
Theseus, Duke of Athens. Egeus addresses him directly and formally: “my noble lord,” “my gracious Duke.” The Duke is the one person in the room with the power to grant what Egeus wants.
Demand
Egeus invokes “the ancient privilege of Athens”: Hermia must marry Demetrius, or, under the law, face death or a lifelong vow of celibacy.

📚 PART 3 · READ & NOTICE: WHAT THE AUDIENCE SEES A FEW LINES LATER

Theseus sends Egeus and Demetrius off to discuss the matter privately, leaving Lysander and Hermia alone on stage. Read what happens next.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 1, SCENE 1
How now, my love? Why is your cheek so pale?
How chance the roses there do fade so fast?

HERMIA.
Belike for want of rain, which I could well
Beteem them from the tempest of my eyes.

LYSANDER.
Ay me! For aught that I could ever read,
Could ever hear by tale or history,
The course of true love never did run smooth.
Lysander and Hermia, alone together (Act 1, Scene 1)

Egeus called this bewitchment and theft. What the audience actually watches, moments later, is two young people comforting each other and making sense of an old idea together: that true love rarely runs smoothly. Nothing about this scene looks like a trick played on an unharden’d heart. It looks like ordinary affection.

🔮 THE QUESTION THIS MODULE KEEPS ASKING
A play has no narrator to tell you what really happened. Instead, characters narrate events to each other, inside the story, usually because they want something. Egeus wants a legal ruling, so he tells the story as bewitchment and theft. Keep asking, scene by scene: whose account can we trust here, and how would we know?
