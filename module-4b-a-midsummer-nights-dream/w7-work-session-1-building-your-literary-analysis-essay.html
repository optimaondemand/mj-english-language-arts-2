💡 THE SHAPE OF A LITERARY ANALYSIS ESSAY
A literary analysis essay is not a summary of what happened. It is a claim about what the play shows, backed up with evidence from the text and your own explanation of what that evidence means. Here is the shape your essay takes, and which Quick Write each part grows from.
PART 1 · THESIS (from Quick Write 6)
Name whose account of the night in the wood you find most believable, or explain why no single account fully earns your trust. State what the play suggests about why people tell the stories they tell. One clear claim you will defend through the whole essay.
PART 2 · EVIDENCE + EXPLANATION, FIRST PIECE (from Quick Writes 3 and 4)
Point to a specific moment where a character narrates an event to someone else. Cite it in MLA format: Shakespeare’s name plus act.scene. Then explain in your own words what that moment shows about whose story deserves belief. The explanation is the part that does the real work.
PART 3 · EVIDENCE + EXPLANATION, SECOND PIECE (from Quick Writes 4 and 5)
Give a second moment, cited the same way. Explain how it adds to your case, and address one counterclaim: a reason someone might trust a different character’s account instead, and why your case still holds.
PART 4 · CONCLUSION (from Quick Write 6)
Restate your point in fresh words and say what the play suggests, overall, about why people tell the stories they tell. Do not just repeat the thesis sentence.
📜 CITING THE PLAY: AUTHOR, ACT.SCENE FORMAT

In an essay about a play, you cite Shakespeare’s name plus act and scene, separated by a period, instead of a page number. This works the same way no matter which printed edition you use, because act and scene divisions stay the same across editions while page numbers do not.

How to read a citation: (Shakespeare, 1.1) means Shakespeare, Act 1, Scene 1. If you already name Shakespeare in your sentence, just give the act and scene: (1.1). If you quote a short phrase directly, put the citation right after the closing quotation mark, before the period that ends your sentence. If you describe a moment in your own words instead of quoting it, the citation still goes at the end of the sentence.

Example: Egeus asks Theseus for “the ancient privilege of Athens” to decide his daughter’s fate (Shakespeare, 1.1).

Use exactly this format for every piece of evidence in your essay, whether you quote directly or describe a moment in your own words. At the end of your essay, add one Works Cited entry: Shakespeare, William. A Midsummer Night’s Dream.

📚 READ & NOTICE: A WORKED EXAMPLE

Here is a short literary analysis of a different question about the play: whether the mechanicals’ performance of Pyramus and Thisbe succeeds or fails as storytelling. Notice how each labeled part matches the shape above. You will not copy these details; this is a model of the moves, not an answer to borrow.

THESIS The mechanicals’ telling of Pyramus and Thisbe fails as the story they intended, but it succeeds as a lesson in how badly a story can be told when the teller does not trust the audience to imagine anything on their own.

EVIDENCE 1 Snout, playing Wall, explains at length that he represents a wall and describes the chink in the wall rather than simply standing in as one (Shakespeare, 5.1). (Then the explanation:) This shows the mechanicals do not trust their audience to accept a simple symbol. Every detail is over-explained, which drains the story of the tension it needs.

EVIDENCE 2 Theseus and his court laugh and interrupt throughout the performance, at one point noting that “the best in this kind are but shadows” (Shakespeare, 5.1). (Then the explanation:) This shows that the audience’s own imagination has to complete a story; a poorly told tale asks too much of its listeners and gives them too little to work with.

CONCLUSION By making the mechanicals’ play fail so completely, the scene argues that a story only works when the teller trusts the listener to meet it partway.

Notice that every piece of evidence is followed immediately by an explanation, and every citation uses author plus act.scene format. That combination is what earns credit, not the detail alone.

⚡ TRY IT · NAME EACH PART OF THE ESSAY

Below are sentences from a literary analysis essay, out of order. For each one, decide which part it is: thesis, evidence, explanation, or conclusion. Decide first, then choose.

1. “This reveals that Helena’s account of her friendship with Hermia is shaped by what she needs to argue in the moment, not by a neutral memory of the past.”
2. “Among all the accounts given at the end of the play, Bottom’s honest admission that he cannot explain his own vision is the most trustworthy, because he never pretends to understand more than he does.”
3. “In the end, the play suggests that people tell stories to make sense of what frightens or confuses them, and that a story’s honesty often shows in what it admits it cannot explain.”
4. “Egeus tells Theseus that Lysander has ‘bewitch’d the bosom’ of his daughter (Shakespeare, 1.1).”
🧠 NOW BUILD YOUR PLAN

Open your Quick Writes and your Reading Journal. In your notebook or in a new doc, fill in the four parts using your own position and your own citations:

Thesis: pull your claim from Quick Write 6. Make sure it names whose account you find most believable, or explains why no account is fully trustworthy, and says something about why people tell stories.
Evidence 1 and its explanation: take your strongest moment from Quick Writes 3 or 4. Cite it in MLA format (Shakespeare’s name plus act.scene) and write one sentence that says what it shows about whose story to trust.
Evidence 2, its explanation, and a counterclaim: add a second cited moment from Quick Writes 4 or 5. Explain it, then name one reason someone might disagree with your case and say why your case still holds.
Conclusion: use your Quick Write 6 intro to restate the point in fresh words, then add one sentence about what the play suggests about storytelling as a whole.
