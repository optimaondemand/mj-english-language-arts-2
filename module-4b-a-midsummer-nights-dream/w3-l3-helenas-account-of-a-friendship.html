Lesson 3.03
Helena's Account of a Friendship
A Narrated Past Used as Evidence
⏱ ~15 min
📝 TODAY'S FOCUS
Helena believes her three friends have all turned on her. To argue against it, she does not just say "stop." She tells a story: the history of her childhood friendship with Hermia. Today you will look at how she uses a memory as evidence in a present argument, the same move Egeus and Titania have already made earlier in the play.

1
Summarize the childhood friendship Helena describes, in your own words.
2
Identify the figurative language Helena uses to describe the friendship, and explain what it means.
3
Explain why Helena tells this story now, and what she wants it to prove.

📚 FROM THE PLAY

Helena has just accused Hermia, Lysander, and Demetrius of mocking her together. Here is how she makes her case.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 3, SCENE 2
Is all the counsel that we two have shar’d,
The sisters’ vows, the hours that we have spent,
When we have chid the hasty-footed time
For parting us—O, is all forgot?
All school-days’ friendship, childhood innocence?
We, Hermia, like two artificial gods,
Have with our needles created both one flower,
Both on one sampler, sitting on one cushion,
Both warbling of one song, both in one key,
As if our hands, our sides, voices, and minds,
Had been incorporate. So we grew together,
Like to a double cherry, seeming parted,
But yet a union in partition,
Two lovely berries moulded on one stem;
So, with two seeming bodies, but one heart;
Two of the first, like coats in heraldry,
Due but to one, and crownèd with one crest.
Helena, believing Hermia has joined the others in mocking her, calls up their shared childhood as proof of a bond that should not be broken.
📚 A STORY USED AS EVIDENCE

Helena compares the two girls to "a double cherry, seeming parted, but yet a union in partition" and to "two lovely berries moulded on one stem." Both are metaphors: two things that look separate but actually grew from one place. She is arguing that she and Hermia are still that close, even though right now it looks like Hermia has abandoned her.

Notice this is the same move you have seen elsewhere in this play: someone narrates a past event to support a claim they are making right now. Egeus narrated Lysander's courtship to win a legal ruling. Titania narrated a story about the changeling boy's mother to justify keeping him. Now Helena narrates her own childhood to try to stop what she believes is a betrayal. A narrated past is being used as evidence, and just like those other moments, it is worth asking: is this a fair and accurate memory, or a memory shaped to win the argument?
