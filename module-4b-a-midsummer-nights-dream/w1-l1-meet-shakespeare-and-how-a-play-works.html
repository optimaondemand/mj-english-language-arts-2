📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A story on a page and a play on a stage are built differently. Today you learn how a play actually works, and you meet the man who wrote this one.
You are about to start A Midsummer Night’s Dream, a comedy written by William Shakespeare. Before you read a single scene, you need to know how a play is built: what counts as dialogue, what counts as a stage direction, and why some characters speak in verse while others speak in ordinary prose. Those choices are not decoration. They are how a playwright builds character and staging with no narrator to explain anything.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell the difference between dialogue and a stage direction on a play script.
2
I will be able to explain how a playwright uses dialogue and staging, not narration, to develop a character.
3
I will be able to tell verse from prose in a script and explain who tends to speak in each.

💡 A NOVEL VERSUS A PLAY
A Novel
A narrator describes what characters look like, think, and feel. The narrator can tell you directly what is happening inside someone’s head.
A Play
There is no narrator. You only get what characters say (dialogue) and short instructions for the actors and stage crew (stage directions). Everything else, you infer.
Because a playwright cannot narrate, character has to be built entirely through what people say, how they say it, and what happens on the stage around them.

📚 PART 2 · READ & NOTICE: MEET WILLIAM SHAKESPEARE

William Shakespeare was born in 1564 in Stratford-upon-Avon, England, and died in 1616. He worked in London as an actor and a playwright for a theater company that later became known as the King’s Men. Over his career he wrote roughly three dozen plays, along with poems and sonnets. A Midsummer Night’s Dream is a comedy, probably written in the middle of the 1590s. It weaves together three groups of characters: the royal court of Athens, a band of amateur actors preparing a play of their own, and a group of fairies living in the wood outside the city.

Shakespeare wrote in the English of his own time, which is why some words and spellings look unfamiliar today. Look for elisions: shortened forms like o’er (over) or bewitch’d (bewitched), where a letter is dropped to keep the rhythm of a line steady. You will meet several of these starting today.

📚 PART 3 · READ & NOTICE: STAGE DIRECTIONS, SPEAKER LABELS, AND VERSE

Here is the opening of the play. Notice three things as you read: the bracketed text before the dialogue begins (a stage direction, never spoken aloud), the character’s name printed in capital letters before each speech (the speaker label), and the fact that a new line of dialogue starts with a capital letter even in the middle of a sentence. That last habit is a sign of verse: poetry written for the stage.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 1, SCENE 1
[Setting: Athens. A room in the Palace of Theseus]


Enter Theseus, Hippolyta, Philostrate and Attendants.

THESEUS.
Now, fair Hippolyta, our nuptial hour
Draws on apace; four happy days bring in
Another moon; but oh, methinks, how slow
This old moon wanes! She lingers my desires,
Like to a step-dame or a dowager,
Long withering out a young man’s revenue.

HIPPOLYTA.
Four days will quickly steep themselves in night;
Four nights will quickly dream away the time;
And then the moon, like to a silver bow
New bent in heaven, shall behold the night
Of our solemnities.
Theseus and Hippolyta open the play (Act 1, Scene 1)

Theseus, the Duke of Athens, and Hippolyta, his bride-to-be, speak in verse from their very first lines. That tells an audience something before a single event has happened: these are important, formal people, and their speech has weight and ceremony.

📚 PART 4 · READ & NOTICE: WHEN CHARACTERS SPEAK IN PROSE

Now compare that to a scene happening the same day, across town. A group of craftsmen (a carpenter, a weaver, a bellows-mender, and others) are casting a play of their own to perform at the Duke’s wedding. Read how they talk.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 1, SCENE 2
Is all our company here?

BOTTOM.
You were best to call them generally, man by man, according to the
scrip.

QUINCE.
Here is the scroll of every man’s name, which is thought fit through
all Athens, to play in our interlude before the Duke and Duchess, on
his wedding-day at night.

BOTTOM.
First, good Peter Quince, say what the play treats on; then read the
names of the actors; and so grow to a point.
Quince and Bottom cast their play (Act 1, Scene 2)

No verse rhythm here. This is prose: ordinary sentences, the way people actually talk. Shakespeare gives his working-class characters prose and his nobles verse far more often than not. That single choice about form, before any character says a clever line, already tells the audience who holds status in this world and who does not. This is exactly what it means for a playwright to develop character through dialogue and staging: the very shape of a character’s speech is doing work.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · USING AI TO LOOK THINGS UP
An AI tool can summarize who Shakespeare was or define the word “prose” in seconds, and that is a reasonable way to check a fact. It cannot read a scene for you and notice, on its own, why a particular character is speaking in verse instead of prose at a particular moment. That noticing is your work as a reader, and it is the skill this module keeps building.
