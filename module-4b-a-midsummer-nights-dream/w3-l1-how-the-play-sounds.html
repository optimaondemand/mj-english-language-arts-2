Lesson 3.01
How the Play Sounds
Blank Verse, Rhymed Couplets, and Prose
⏱ ~20 min
📝 TODAY'S FOCUS
Shakespeare does not write every line of this play the same way. He changes the sound of the language depending on who is speaking and how serious the moment is. Today you will learn to name and hear three different forms: blank verse, rhymed couplets, and prose. All three show up in Act 3, sometimes on the same page.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
Name and define blank verse, rhymed couplets, and prose, using real lines from Act 3.
2
Explain what a change in form tells you about who is speaking and how serious the moment is.
3
Write four lines of your own in rhymed couplets, using a steady beat and a planned rhyme.

📚 FORM 1 · BLANK VERSE

Meter is the pattern of beats in a line. Most of the serious speeches in this play, the court, the lovers arguing, keep a steady five-beat meter in every line. Blank verse means the lines have that steady meter but do NOT rhyme. It sounds like careful, elevated speech, not like a song.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 3, SCENE 2
Why should you think that I should woo in scorn?
Scorn and derision never come in tears.
Look when I vow, I weep; and vows so born,
In their nativity all truth appears.
How can these things in me seem scorn to you,
Bearing the badge of faith, to prove them true?
Lysander, defending himself to Helena. No two line-endings rhyme: scorn/tears, born/appears, you/true. The lines still keep a steady five-beat rhythm.

Say the lines out loud. You can hear the beat holding steady across all six lines, but no line-ending matches another. That is blank verse: shape and control, without a rhyme. The play uses this same form for Theseus and Egeus in the opening court scene, and for most of the lovers’ arguing throughout the play.

📚 FORM 2 · RHYMED COUPLETS

A rhymed couplet is a pair of lines, back to back, that rhyme with each other (an AABB pattern). The fairies almost always speak this way, and the human lovers switch into it at their most heightened, emotional moments. The rhyme makes the speech sound extra formal, extra certain, sometimes even a little magical.

Example A, a fairy speaking:
📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 3, SCENE 1
Out of this wood do not desire to go.
Thou shalt remain here whether thou wilt or no.
I am a spirit of no common rate.
The summer still doth tend upon my state;
Titania, enchanted, speaking to Bottom. go/no and rate/state: two rhymed pairs in a row.
Example B, a human lover at a big moment:
📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 3, SCENE 2
You are unkind, Demetrius; be not so,
For you love Hermia; this you know I know.
And here, with all good will, with all my heart,
In Hermia’s love I yield you up my part;
And yours of Helena to me bequeath,
Whom I do love and will do till my death.
Lysander, making a grand offer to Demetrius. so/know, heart/part, bequeath/death: three rhymed pairs in a row, right at an emotional high point.

Notice the pattern in both: line 1 rhymes with line 2, then line 3 rhymes with line 4, and so on. When Shakespeare drops rhyme in for a stretch like this, it is a signal: pay attention, this moment matters more than the ones around it.

📚 FORM 3 · PROSE

Prose is ordinary language with no set beat and no rhyme, the way people actually talk. In this play, the workmen almost always speak in prose, even when everyone around them is speaking in verse. Read how Bottom and his friends plan their play.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 3, SCENE 1
Are we all met?

QUINCE.
Pat, pat; and here’s a marvellous convenient place for our rehearsal.
This green plot shall be our stage, this hawthorn brake our
tiring-house; and we will do it in action, as we will do it before the
Duke.

BOTTOM.
Peter Quince?

QUINCE.
What sayest thou, bully Bottom?

BOTTOM.
There are things in this comedy of Pyramus and Thisbe that will never
please. First, Pyramus must draw a sword to kill himself; which the
ladies cannot abide. How answer you that?

SNOUT
By’r lakin, a parlous fear.

STARVELING.
I believe we must leave the killing out, when all is done.
Bottom, Quince, Snout, and Starveling, planning their play. Ordinary talk. No beat to count, no rhymes to find.

There is no beat to tap and no rhyme to find. It reads like a real conversation between working men solving a practical problem. Shakespeare uses prose here to mark the workmen as ordinary, lower-status characters, and it is also part of what makes them funny: they discuss a magical play in the plainest possible language.

💡 THE THREE FORMS, SIDE BY SIDE
Blank Verse
Steady five-beat meter. No rhyme.

Who: the court, the lovers arguing seriously.
Signals: this is a serious, human-scale moment.
Rhymed Couplets
Paired rhymes, AABB.

Who: the fairies, and lovers at a heightened moment.
Signals: magic, formality, or extra emotional weight.
Prose
No beat. No rhyme. Ordinary talk.

Who: the workmen (the mechanicals).
Signals: lower status, comedy, plain practical talk.

📘 READ & NOTICE

As you read Act 3, Scene 1 today, watch for the moment the form shifts, from the workmen’s prose, to Titania’s rhymed couplets once she wakes enchanted.

Add this to the Week 3 section of your Reading Journal: find one more short passage in Scene 1 and name its form (blank verse, rhymed couplets, or prose). Explain in one or two sentences what that form choice tells you about the speaker or the moment.

✏️ YOUR TURN: WRITE FOUR LINES IN RHYMED COUPLETS

Pick any topic you like: a pet, a food, the weather, a sport, anything. Write four lines (two couplets, AABB) about it. Here is the full process, shown openly.

STEP 1 · A WORKED EXAMPLE (topic: rain)
The rain comes down and taps the glass,
While puddles form upon the grass.
The clouds roll by, then drift away,
And bring the sunlight back to play.
Rhyme pairs: glass / grass (AA), away / play (BB). Each line runs about eight beats. That is the whole recipe.
STEP 2 · BEAT COUNT
Say each of your lines out loud. Count how many strong beats you hear. Pick a number between six and ten beats, and try to keep it about the same across all four lines. You do not need to be exact. A steady, roughly even beat is the goal, not a perfect count.
STEP 3 · RHYME-PAIR PLANNER
Line 1 ends with: ______________
Line 2 ends with a word that rhymes with Line 1: ______________
Line 3 ends with: ______________
Line 4 ends with a word that rhymes with Line 3: ______________
Fill in your two rhyme pairs first. Then build the rest of each line backward from the rhyming word.

Write your finished four lines in the Week 3 section of your Reading Journal, under your Read & Notice entry.
