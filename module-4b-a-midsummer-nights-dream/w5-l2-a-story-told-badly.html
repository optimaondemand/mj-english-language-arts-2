Lesson 5.02
A Story Told Badly
Lesson
⏱ ~14 min
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A story can fail in specific, nameable ways. Today a group of amateur actors do almost everything wrong, and you will use their mistakes to see what strong storytelling avoids.
Theseus has chosen to watch a play performed by “hard-handed men that work in Athens,” a group of workmen putting on the story of Pyramus and Thisbe. Their telling of it is so clumsy that the clumsiness itself becomes the entertainment. Watching them fail is a fast way to learn what a well-told story does instead.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name three specific ways a story can fail: over-explaining, breaking the illusion, and narrating instead of showing.
2
I will be able to point to a line from the mechanicals' play and explain which mistake it makes.
3
I will be able to rewrite a badly told moment so that it shows the audience something instead of explaining it to them.
📚 PART 1 · READ & NOTICE: WARM-UP
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Has anyone ever told you a joke and then explained why it was funny right afterward? What did the explanation do to the joke? (No wrong answer.)
📚 PART 2 · READ & NOTICE: MISTAKE ONE, OVER-EXPLAINING

Before the play begins, a Prologue enters to set the scene. Read what he says.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 5, SCENE 1
Gentles, perchance you wonder at this show;
But wonder on, till truth make all things plain.
This man is Pyramus, if you would know;
This beauteous lady Thisbe is certain.
This man, with lime and rough-cast, doth present
Wall, that vile wall which did these lovers sunder;
And through Wall’s chink, poor souls, they are content
To whisper, at the which let no man wonder.
This man, with lantern, dog, and bush of thorn,
Presenteth Moonshine, for, if you will know,
By moonshine did these lovers think no scorn
To meet at Ninus’ tomb, there, there to woo.
This grisly beast (which Lion hight by name)
The trusty Thisbe, coming first by night,
Did scare away, or rather did affright;
And as she fled, her mantle she did fall;
Which Lion vile with bloody mouth did stain.
Anon comes Pyramus, sweet youth, and tall,
And finds his trusty Thisbe’s mantle slain;
Whereat with blade, with bloody blameful blade,
He bravely broach’d his boiling bloody breast;
And Thisbe, tarrying in mulberry shade,
His dagger drew, and died. For all the rest,
Let Lion, Moonshine, Wall, and lovers twain,
At large discourse while here they do remain.
The Prologue, introducing the dumb show, Act 5, Scene 1
The actors then enter and act out the whole plot in a silent “dumb show” while the Prologue narrates it, line by line: “This man is Pyramus,” “This beauteous lady Thisbe is certain,” “This man … doth present Wall.” A strong storyteller lets the audience recognize a character through action and dialogue. The Prologue instead announces every fact directly, leaving nothing for the audience to notice on their own. He even gives away the ending before the actors have said a word.
📚 PART 3 · READ & NOTICE: MISTAKE TWO, BREAKING THE ILLUSION

A workman named Snout plays a wall. Read how he introduces his part.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 5, SCENE 1
In this same interlude it doth befall
That I, one Snout by name, present a wall:
And such a wall as I would have you think
That had in it a crannied hole or chink,
Through which the lovers, Pyramus and Thisbe,
Did whisper often very secretly.
This loam, this rough-cast, and this stone, doth show
That I am that same wall; the truth is so:
And this the cranny is, right and sinister,
Through which the fearful lovers are to whisper.
Wall, played by Snout, Act 5, Scene 1
Instead of simply standing in place and letting the audience accept him as a wall, Snout explains the materials he is made of (“loam,” “rough-cast,” and “stone”) and insists, “the truth is so.” A good storyteller invites an audience to imagine along with them. Snout instead keeps interrupting the illusion to prove he is telling the truth, which only reminds everyone that he is an actor holding up his fingers to represent a crack in a wall.
📚 PART 4 · READ & NOTICE: MISTAKE THREE, RUINING THE SUSPENSE

A workman named Snug plays a lion. A lion should be at least a little frightening. Read what he says before he even roars.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 5, SCENE 1
You, ladies, you, whose gentle hearts do fear
The smallest monstrous mouse that creeps on floor,
May now, perchance, both quake and tremble here,
When lion rough in wildest rage doth roar.
Then know that I, one Snug the joiner, am
A lion fell, nor else no lion’s dam;
For if I should as lion come in strife
Into this place, ’twere pity on my life.
Lion, played by Snug, Act 5, Scene 1
Snug is so worried about scaring the ladies in the audience that he announces, before doing anything lion-like at all, that he is really “Snug the joiner” and not a real lion. A storyteller who wants an audience to feel real suspense has to let them feel it. Snug removes the suspense before it can even start by explaining it away in advance.
🎯 TRY IT · NAME THE MISTAKE
⚡ TRY IT
Match each moment to the storytelling mistake it makes.
Snout announces that the loam, rough-cast, and stone prove he really is a wall.
Snug tells the ladies he is really “Snug the joiner” and not a real lion.
The Prologue narrates the entire plot, including the ending, while the actors silently act it out.
📚 NOTICE THE CALLBACK

Watching the play, Hippolyta and Theseus have a short exchange that echoes Day 1's lesson.

📜 FROM THE PLAY · ACT 5, SCENE 1
This is the silliest stuff that ever I heard.

THESEUS.
The best in this kind are but shadows; and the worst are no worse, if
imagination amend them.

HIPPOLYTA.
It must be your imagination then, and not theirs.
Hippolyta and Theseus, watching the play
The same word from Day 1 returns: imagination. This time Theseus uses it to defend the mechanicals, not to doubt the lovers. A weak performance, he says, only becomes a disaster if the audience's own imagination makes it worse than it is.
