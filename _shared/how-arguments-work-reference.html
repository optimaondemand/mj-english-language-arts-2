How Arguments Work
Three ways people reason, and how to judge them
⏱ ~12 min
📖 WHY THIS PAGE EXISTS
Characters in the books you read spend a great deal of time trying to convince each other. So will people you meet for the rest of your life.
This page gives you three names for three ways of reasoning. Once you have them, you will start seeing them everywhere: in Gulliver’s attempts to impress a king, in a character defending a choice, in an advertisement, in an argument at your own dinner table. You will come back to this page all year. Bookmark it.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS PAGE YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell the difference between reasoning from a rule, reasoning from examples, and reasoning to the likeliest explanation.
2
I will be able to say what makes each kind strong and where each one tends to break.
3
I will be able to use four questions to weigh any argument a character makes in the books we read.
💡 THE ONE IDEA TO HOLD ONTO · THREE WAYS TO REASON
Every argument moves in one of three directions. Learn the direction, not just the label.
🔑
Deductive: from the rule down
You start with a rule everyone accepts, add a fact, and the conclusion has to follow. If the rule and the fact are true, the conclusion cannot be false.
All whales are mammals. A beluga is a whale. So a beluga is a mammal.
📈
Inductive: from examples up
You start with things you have actually seen and reason toward a general conclusion. More examples make it stronger, but it is never airtight.
The bakery has had a line out the door every morning this month. So the bakery is probably popular.
🔍
Abductive: the best available guess
You see something and reason back to the likeliest explanation. Detectives do this. It is useful, and it is the easiest of the three to get wrong.
The kitchen floor is wet and the window is open. It probably rained in.
🔍 WHERE THE THIRD KIND GOES WRONG

Reasoning to the likeliest explanation is genuinely useful. It is also the one that slides most easily into a bad conclusion, because a guess can feel finished before it has been checked.

Watch this one slip:
“My grandparents all have gray hair. So all elderly people have gray hair.”

Three people is not enough people. The guess ran straight past “probably” and landed on “all.” Watch for this overreach as you read this year, and expect to meet it head on when we look at weak reasoning during The Call of the Wild. A good explanation stays a candidate until you have checked it against more than the handful of cases that suggested it.

💡 THE TEST THAT CATCHES IT
Ask: what else would explain this just as well? If you can name a second explanation the speaker never considered, the argument is not finished yet.
⚡ TRY IT · WHICH DIRECTION IS THE REASONING GOING?

Read each one and decide which way it moves. Decide in your head before you choose.

1. “It has frozen every morning this week, so it will probably freeze tomorrow.”
2. “The plate is empty and the dog looks guilty. The dog ate it.”
3. “Every student in this course keeps a Reading Journal. Maya is in this course. So Maya keeps a Reading Journal.”
4. “The two books we have read this year both end sadly, so this author’s books always end sadly.”
📝 THE FOUR QUESTIONS · USE THESE ALL YEAR

Whenever a character in one of our books tries to convince someone, ask these four. You will see them again in every module.

  1. What are they trying to get? Name what the speaker wants the listener to believe or do.
  2. What are they resting it on? A rule, a set of examples, or a guess about a cause?
  3. Who are they talking to? People shape an argument around the person in front of them. Notice what the speaker assumes that listener already believes.
  4. What did they leave out? Name one thing that would weaken the argument, and ask why it never came up.
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

You now have three names and four questions. That is enough to start taking arguments apart. The next time a character in one of our books tries to win someone over, you will not just notice that they are arguing. You will be able to say how, and whether it holds.
